Media Mogotsi Testimony Reveals New Detail on Dossier Naming Matlala

November 19, 2025 ● Tebuho Zongwana

Businessman Brown Mogotsi has delivered extensive testimony explaining how he came into possession of a 2018 intelligence dossier that named Vusimuzi Cat Matlala as the alleged John Wick killer. His testimony has opened new questions about the circulation of sensitive intelligence documents, the relationships between private businessmen and law enforcement, and how such allegations were handled within government structures.

Mogotsi told the court that the dossier reached him through intermediaries who claimed the information was urgent and needed to be taken to the highest level of policing. The document included names of suspects, descriptions of violent incidents linked to organised crime, and commentary suggesting that Matlala was involved in targeted killings that had shaken parts of Gauteng. According to Mogotsi, the dossier read like a combination of intelligence notes, surveillance summaries, and unverified tips gathered by sources who claimed to have direct knowledge of criminal networks.

Mogotsi said he was shocked to see Matlala’s name included so prominently. The two men had known each other for years through overlapping business environments in Gauteng. Although they were not formal business partners, their circles often intersected. Mogotsi described Matlala as a figure who moved between legitimate business settings and spaces where powerful individuals, political actors, and criminal networks operated in close proximity.

He testified that their relationship was not deeply personal but one built on years of encounters at business gatherings, community meetings, and informal engagements where government aligned individuals and private players frequently interacted. Mogotsi emphasised that he had no personal grievance against Matlala and that his decision to escalate the dossier was rooted in concern about the seriousness of the allegations.

However, he admitted to the court that he did not receive any follow up communication from the ministry or law enforcement confirming whether the information had been investigated. The absence of feedback raises questions about how intelligence was handled at the time, and whether the dossier was acted upon, shelved, or dismissed as unreliable.

Background on Matlala

Vusimuzi Cat Matlala has been referenced in various accounts as a powerful figure known in circles that straddle both formal and informal economies. Reports have linked him to groups that relied on targeted violence. The John Wick label is associated with a series of calculated attacks believed to be connected to disputes in the organised crime environment. While Matlala has been mentioned in intelligence discussions, official records have not demonstrated that he was charged specifically based on this dossier.

Ties to government and broader implications

Mogotsi acknowledged that both he and Matlala had indirect links to government associates. These connections were described as typical of the Gauteng business landscape where political influence and private interests are intertwined.

His testimony has revived debate over the blurred lines between business networks, political access, and the handling of criminal intelligence in South Africa. Questions now arise about who authorised the creation of the dossier, why it was circulated outside formal police channels, and how a businessman came to serve as a courier for such sensitive information.

Investigators are expected to follow up on the origin of the document, its credibility, and the decisions taken by senior officials after its handover. The court will continue to examine whether the dossier forms part of a broader pattern of intelligence handling that may have influenced later investigations and public security operations.

As the case unfolds, the testimony is drawing attention to the complex web of relationships connecting private individuals, government aligned networks, and those accused of violent criminal activity. The implications may extend beyond Matlala, potentially exposing gaps in how intelligence is managed and how the state responds to high-risk claims involving influential figures.
