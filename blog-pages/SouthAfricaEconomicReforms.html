Business South Africa’s Reforms Fall Short of Growth Goals

October 15, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa is making some progress with economic reforms, but according to Moody’s Ratings, it’s just not enough to reach the government’s ambitious goal of 3.5% growth by 2030. The country has been stuck in a low growth cycle for over a decade, averaging less than 1% per year. The reasons are well known: unreliable electricity supply, poor infrastructure, problems with logistics, and deep rooted issues like crime and corruption.

Following the last national elections, the African National Congress lost its majority, and a coalition government was formed. This new administration has been trying to push reforms forward to boost growth. But Moody’s isn’t convinced that the current pace and depth of these reforms will be enough to lift the country’s potential growth rate beyond 2%. Evan Wohlmann, a senior credit officer at Moody’s, made it clear that even with ongoing efforts, South Africa’s economy is still falling behind. A study by Investec Wealth & Investment found that if the country had grown consistently at 4.5% since 2010 like many of its emerging market peers it would now be about 37% larger.

For now, most economists expect the economy to grow by somewhere between 0.8% and 1.2% in 2025, with hopes it could edge slightly above 1.5% in 2026. There was a bit of good news recently: GDP growth in the second quarter of 2025 came in at 0.8%, beating the 0.5% forecast. That followed a weak first quarter of just 0.1%. This small surprise has provided a little optimism, especially since the impact of global trade tensions and U.S. tariffs hasn’t yet hit the industrial sector as hard as feared. On the consumer side, there’s been some support from rising wages and lower interest rates, which have kept household spending going, even though many households are still under pressure.

Still, it’s clear that no one expects growth to bounce back to 3% anytime soon especially not without major structural reforms. Moody’s projects 1% growth for 2025 and 1.6% in 2026. That slow pace is going to make it harder for the government to rein in debt, which is already a major concern. According to Wohlmann, without faster growth, it’s going to be very difficult for South Africa to lower its debt meaningfully. That, in turn, weakens the country’s chances of regaining an investment grade credit rating something it lost in March 2020 when Moody’s downgraded it to sub investment (junk) status. Right now, South Africa’s rating sits at Ba2 with a stable outlook, and Moody’s next review is scheduled for December 5. If the country doesn’t manage to speed up reform or boost growth, it risks a further downgrade.

One major drag on the economy is weak investment, especially in infrastructure. Foreign direct investment dropped to just $2.5 billion in 2024, the lowest in seven years, and almost 30% less than in 2023. Moody’s notes that without a meaningful uptick in private infrastructure investment, growth is unlikely to break past the 2% ceiling. Wohlmann warned that if the outlook for economic growth worsens whether due to reform setbacks or deteriorating government finances the country could face renewed pressure on its credit rating. In short, unless South Africa finds a way to unlock higher growth and fix its underlying issues, its economic and fiscal challenges are likely to persist.
