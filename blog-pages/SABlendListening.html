Media South Africans Blend Traditional and Digital Listening

July 29, 2025 ● TNJ Reporter

Radio in South Africa is undergoing a quiet but powerful transformation. While it still plays a big role in people’s lives especially through traditional broadcasts digital platforms are changing the way people tune in. These shifts are not just about convenience; they reflect a deeper evolution in how we consume audio. People now expect more choice, control, and tailored experiences when they listen.

From April to June 2025, infoQuest ran a nationwide survey of 1,000 radio listeners across different ages, backgrounds, and preferences. At the same time, Media Host Group tested a passive listening tool that could track real-time listening habits using audio recognition tech. This mix of survey responses and behavioural data gave a clearer picture of how people are interacting with both traditional and streaming radio today.

What stood out most is how easily listeners are moving between the old and the new. Some stick to FM radio, especially older or Afrikaans-speaking listeners, but many are blending both. Over 60% use streaming platforms, either alongside or instead of traditional radio. It’s clear that streaming is no longer just an add-on it’s becoming part of the core listening experience.

When it comes to how much time people actually spend listening, the majority keep it under two hours a day, although about 20% are heavy listeners who go beyond five hours. Most people still listen nearly every day of the week. Whether it’s for a quick update or background noise while working, radio continues to find a place in daily routines.

Interestingly, both streaming and terrestrial radio show similar listening patterns in terms of duration, with shorter sessions being the most common. Streaming tends to attract more casual, quick listeners, while longer sessions are still more common with traditional radio. Even so, the differences aren’t huge, which suggests that listening habits might be more influenced by time constraints or context than the platform itself.

The most popular time to listen is in the early morning between 5 and 9 AM when 72% of people are tuned in. This fits with habits like catching up on news, traffic, or simply easing into the day. The late afternoon and early evening (4 to 8 PM) is another peak period, likely during the commute home or while unwinding after work. Midday listening is also solid, showing that radio stays relevant throughout the day.

Streaming platforms are becoming serious players. DStv Audio Channels, YouTube, and Spotify are the top platforms people use. Many also stream directly from radio station websites, showing that there's still demand for direct content from broadcasters. Podcast platforms are emerging but not yet dominant.

The average streaming listener uses about three platforms, which points to a scattered attention span and the desire to mix and match depending on the type of content they’re after whether that’s music, talk shows, or podcasts. This fragmentation has big implications for advertisers and content creators. Listeners are not loyal to one platform or even one station.

They jump between services, curating their own audio experiences based on mood, time of day, or convenience. Media Host’s tech-backed pilot confirmed this trend, showing that even within short listening windows, users frequently switch between platforms.

Most listening happens at home, with 80% tuning in from their living spaces. Car listening is still strong, with 62% listening while driving. About a third of people also listen at work, despite possible restrictions, proving that radio continues to fit into various parts of life.

Smartphones are now the most common device for radio listening. This shift highlights how central mobile devices have become in media consumption. Apps and browsers are now key gateways to radio, and Media Host’s passive listening app picked up listening in many different settings from home to car to public spaces.

Still, traditional devices like home radios and car stereos remain important, especially for certain segments of the population. When it comes to stations, Metro FM leads the pack with half of the respondents tuning in regularly. Radio 2000 and Gagasi FM also have strong followings.

Beyond that, there’s a long tail of stations national, regional, and genre-specific each carving out its own loyal audience. On average, listeners follow about four different stations, suggesting a desire for variety and content that fits different needs and moods.

In all, the data paints a picture of a vibrant but fragmented audio landscape. People are engaging with radio more flexibly than ever before, jumping between platforms, stations, and devices based on what suits them best in the moment. For anyone in the audio space from advertisers to broadcasters keeping up with these changing habits is crucial to staying relevant in an increasingly digital world.
