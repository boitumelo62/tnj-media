Business South Africa’s Fruit Exports Shift Focus to China and Middle East

August 12, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa is shifting its focus to new export markets in Asia and beyond as it deals with steep US import tariffs that have hit its agricultural sector hard. Agriculture Minister John Steenhuisen says the country’s top priority right now is boosting exports to China, where South Africa has secured duty-free access for five types of fruit though he didn’t name which ones.

Beyond China, South Africa is also eyeing markets in Russia, South Korea, the Philippines, Japan, and several Middle Eastern countries. Steenhuisen was clear about his approach: if there’s demand, South Africa is ready to sell. “I would sell my products anywhere in the world, to whoever wants them,” he said in an interview, adding that the government’s job is to find buyers and open up trade protocols wherever possible.

The urgency comes after many South African exports to the US including citrus, grapes, nuts, and wine were slapped with 30% import duties starting on August 7. It’s the harshest tariff regime the US has imposed on any country in sub-Saharan Africa, part of a broader move by former President Donald Trump to rework American trade relationships.

Despite the setback, Steenhuisen says some local producers can still compete. Thanks to a weak rand, the exchange rate actually works in their favor. For example, avocado growers have told him they’re willing to take on the 30% tariff as long as they can still access the US market.

At the same time, Steenhuisen is working to fix a long-running issue between South Africa’s citrus industry and the European Union over health protocols. He’s pushing for a bilateral agreement that would allow South African fruit to bypass certain restrictions.

The citrus industry has long criticized these EU measures, saying they cost the sector around R3.7 billion every season. Resolving the issue could unlock a major opportunity. “The low-hanging fruit for us is to get this black spot issue resolved in Europe,” he said. “We could then redirect a lot of the citrus in there.”
