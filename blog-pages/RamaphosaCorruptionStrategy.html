Business What Ramaphosa’s Strategy Means for Investors

September 8, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

President Cyril Ramaphosa acknowledges that South Africans are eager to see more decisive action against corruption, including more arrests and prosecutions. However, he points out that a lot of important work is happening quietly behind the scenes, without much public attention. The government has faced strong criticism for how it has handled corruption, especially following the findings of the State Capture Commission.

This commission, which operated from 2018 to 2021, uncovered widespread and deep rooted corruption involving billions of rands. It revealed schemes like kickbacks, fraud related to government contracts, and dealings tied to politically connected individuals and groups. More than 1,500 people were implicated in the commission’s findings, but only a handful have been arrested, and even fewer have been successfully prosecuted. Ramaphosa has also been criticized for having some of those implicated still serving in his cabinet and government.

Despite this, in his weekly letter to the public, the president emphasized that fighting corruption isn’t just about putting people behind bars. It’s about completely overhauling the institutions that have allowed corruption to thrive. He said real progress is being made in this regard. Ramaphosa acknowledged the public’s desire for more convictions, especially for those linked to state capture, but stressed that the fight against corruption is much broader. Corruption isn’t just the actions of a few bad actors; it can become part of the culture within government agencies, state owned companies, and businesses. The true victory comes from stopping corruption before it starts.

Regarding arrests, Ramaphosa highlighted that law enforcement agencies like the Hawks and the Special Investigating Unit are actively working on investigations into corruption in state companies such as the South African Post Office, Postbank, and the South African Social Security Agency. Other investigations involve financial misconduct in water entities, corrupt fuel tenders, and municipal graft. He noted that this year alone has seen arrests related to corruption in major institutions like Eskom, the police, Transnet, and various municipalities.

While these allegations are deeply troubling, the president said it’s encouraging that the corruption has been identified and that legal steps are being taken. Ramaphosa also pointed to ongoing reforms aimed at addressing the recommendations from the State Capture Commission, even though it’s been three years since those recommendations were made.

One key development is the work of the National Anti Corruption Advisory Council (NACAC), which was established in 2022 to guide the country’s anti corruption strategy. The council recently handed over its final report, recommending the creation of a permanent, independent anti corruption body called the Office of Public Integrity and Anti Corruption. This body would be responsible not only for investigating corruption but also for preventing it.

The council’s report also suggested ways to improve coordination among law enforcement agencies, use artificial intelligence to help prevent corruption, and establish a system for sharing anti corruption data. Ramaphosa said these recommendations are now under review by government institutions and will be brought to cabinet for discussion.

He concluded by saying that the fight against corruption is complicated and long term. It requires breaking down deeply entrenched systems of patronage, strengthening the government’s ability to handle complex cases, and closing loopholes that have allowed corruption to thrive.
