Business South Africa Risks Losing Key US Market Access

September 23, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South African officials and business leaders are growing increasingly uneasy as a vital trade agreement with the United States is about to expire, and Washington has offered no clarity on whether it will be renewed. The African Growth and Opportunity Act, known as AGOA, is set to end on September 30. First enacted in 2000, the agreement has played a central role in US Africa trade, allowing 32 eligible African countries, including South Africa, to export thousands of products to the US without paying duties or facing quotas.

In return, countries are expected to follow certain principles like supporting open markets, good governance, and fair access for American investments. The US has regularly reviewed countries’ compliance and has removed some, like Ethiopia, Mali, and Uganda, for falling short. AGOA was last renewed in 2015 for ten years, but despite bipartisan support in Washington, no new extension has been passed. The return of Donald Trump to the White House earlier this year has only added more uncertainty, as he’s known for favoring tariffs and shunning wide-reaching trade deals.

Even before Trump’s return, South Africa’s place in the programme had been under scrutiny. It’s seen as economically stronger than most other African participants and, more recently, its foreign policy alignments with Russia, China, and Iran have drawn criticism from US lawmakers who argue the country is no longer acting in America’s interest.

Still, AGOA’s impact has been undeniable. According to a UN study, US imports from AGOA countries rose 37% between 2001 and 2021 and the number more than doubled when oil imports from Angola and Nigeria were excluded. South Africa has gained the most, exporting an estimated R71.5 billion to the US under AGOA in 2024 alone, nearly half of all its exports to the country.

That figure has climbed from R31 billion in 2019 and R64 billion in 2023, adding up to almost R300 billion over a few years. The agreement has not only boosted trade but also helped African economies secure valuable US dollars essential for international payments. The US Trade Representative’s office has credited AGOA with driving economic growth and encouraging better governance across the continent. On average, US imports under AGOA have been five times higher than the annual aid given to African countries.

Without the duty-free access AGOA provides, many African-made products would become less competitive in the US, which could slash exports, deter investment, and cost thousands of jobs. Early analysis from a London-based consultancy suggests AGOA’s end could shrink Africa’s exports to the US by 25%, amounting to a 1% drop in the continent’s global trade. The African Development Bank has already lowered its growth forecast for Africa, pointing to trade uncertainty and rising tariffs as key reasons.

Kenya’s textile and apparel industry is particularly vulnerable, with 300,000 jobs at risk, according to its Trade Minister. In South Africa, where unemployment remains above 30% and the economy is barely growing, tens of thousands of jobs could also be on the line if AGOA disappears. It’s not just Africa that’s feeling the pressure. US companies are also worried about the fallout. The US Chamber of Commerce has called on Congress to act quickly, warning that letting AGOA lapse would harm American business interests and weaken its influence on the continent.
