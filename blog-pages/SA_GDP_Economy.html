Business Growth Returns to SA Economy Just Not Enough for the People

September 10, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa’s economy is showing some signs of life, but it's still not enough to improve the average person's situation. According to the latest data from Stats SA, the country’s GDP grew by 0.8% in the second quarter of 2025, which was actually better than most economists expected. Compared to the same time last year, growth was 0.6%, and over a six month period, it was 0.7%. Mining and manufacturing helped push the economy forward. The mining sector grew by 3.7%, and manufacturing was up 1.8%.

Together, they played a key role in this modest recovery. Despite the positive momentum, the growth rate is still well below what’s needed to keep up with South Africa’s growing population, which increases by about 1.5% each year. In simple terms, while the economy is growing, it's not growing fast enough to make the average South African better off. As a result, the country is experiencing a per capita recession, which means the economy is growing on paper, but people are getting poorer. Economists had expected weaker numbers.

Reuters had forecasted 0.5% growth for the quarter, while Nedbank was closer with a 0.6% estimate, accurately predicting rebounds in mining and manufacturing after a slow first quarter. Still, even though the second quarter looked better than the first, when growth was just 0.1%, it’s far from enough to shift the broader economic picture. Maarten Ackerman, Citadel’s chief economist, said the latest GDP numbers show that the economy is holding up despite tough conditions both at home and globally. But he also pointed out that the annual growth rate is still around one percentage point below population growth, underlining the structural issues that are holding the country back.

Looking ahead, the rest of the year doesn’t promise much improvement. Thanda Sithole from FNB expects 2025 to end with 1.0% growth, which could rise slightly to 1.4% in 2026 and 1.9% in 2027. While external factors like US tariffs are creating uncertainty, there are a few positives. Inflation is low, interest rates have been cut, and some structural reforms are helping. Strong car sales, especially of cheaper models, are one example of how lower borrowing costs are supporting demand. Agriculture is also doing well thanks to good weather and investment in equipment, and trade continues to grow as wage increases outpace inflation.

Ackerman warns that real progress depends on deeper reforms and more involvement from the private sector. On a more hopeful note, he believes the South African Reserve Bank might consider cutting interest rates soon, given the weak economic growth, low inflation, and similar moves from the US Federal Reserve. South Africa's economy is showing small improvements, but not nearly enough to make a difference in everyday life for most people.
