Media Media Leaders Unite to Shape South Africa’s Digital Future

September 26, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Popular Blogs:

Media

Rethinking Cultural Relevance

Media

From Media Player to Global Pioneer

On Tuesday, 16 September 2025, the Advertising Media Association of South Africa (AMASA), in partnership with the Gordon Institute of Business Science (GIBS), held the AMASA Igniters Forum a much anticipated gathering of media leaders, strategists, and innovators. The central question of the day, Is the media industry genuinely evolving, or are we just dressing up old ideas in digital clothing? Shoeshoe Ntsoaki Qhu, CEO of the Media Development and Diversity Agency, opened the forum with a powerful keynote.

She issued a clear warning South African media is at risk of falling behind because it's moving too slowly in adapting to change. But she also cautioned against rushing blindly ahead. Her message was to "hurry slowly" encouraging innovation while ensuring that no one, especially those who still rely on traditional and community media, is left behind in the digital shift. Throughout the day, discussions zeroed in on the most pressing challenges and opportunities facing the industry. One key issue was the urgent need to invest in community level media, especially platforms that speak to people in their own languages and reflect their local realities.

Another was the importance of developing policies and regulations that can keep up with fast changing technologies a task that can’t be left to government alone. Instead, there was a strong call for collaboration across the industry to create frameworks that support transparency, accountability, and long term growth. The forum also shone a light on the role of small businesses and young entrepreneurs in shaping the future of media. These groups, speakers said, aren’t just important for economic reasons they also bring fresh ideas, creative energy, and a closer connection to grassroots audiences. At the same time, participants stressed that while global standards are crucial, local media players need more support to meet these standards without being pushed out of the game.

The panel of experts brought a wide range of voices to the table from Merissa Himraj of WPP Media, to legal expert Michael Motsoeneng-Bill, branding entrepreneur Pat Mahlangu, and Cheryl Manentsa from Nedbank. Together, they shared insights on everything from media buying to entrepreneurship to corporate strategy. Audience members also raised vital concerns around fact checking, lack of resources, and the need for stronger self regulation within the industry. As the forum came to a close, Memoria Masilela, AMASA chairperson and head of government and media strategy at United Stations, summed up the challenge, the industry must move with the times, not only by embracing new technology and cultural shifts, but also by investing in skills development, mentorship, and growth especially for the youth who will lead the sector into the future.

There was a shared understanding that change can’t just happen at the top it has to be inclusive, grounded in ethical practices, and driven by smart, well informed strategies that help smaller players engage with bigger opportunities. That means making sure media owners especially from smaller or community based outlets are equipped to meet compliance standards and participate in industry proposals confidently and effectively. The AMASA Igniters Forum made it clear, real transformation in media will require collective effort, bold thinking, and sustained collaboration.
