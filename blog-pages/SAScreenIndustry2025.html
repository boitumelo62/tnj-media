Media South Africa’s Screen Industry at a Crossroads as 2025 Draws to a Close

December 15, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

As we close out 2025, South Africa’s film and television industry finds itself in a strange and uneasy place. It is not collapsing, but it is certainly not thriving either. Instead, it feels suspended somewhere in between. Local production has dropped sharply, commissioning has slowed, and the knock on effects have landed hardest on freelancers, actors and crew who survive from contract to contract. For many, this has been a genuinely difficult year, not just financially, but emotionally too.

At the same time, major structural shifts are happening above our heads. One of the most significant is the changing relationship between Netflix and Warner Bros. Almost overnight, African audiences are being flooded with a new wave of Warner Bros and HBO backed content through Netflix, and that fundamentally alters the competitive landscape. For years, platforms like DStv and Canal+ leaned heavily on HBO and Warner Bros titles as premium drivers that kept subscribers loyal. Now those same titles increasingly live on their biggest global competitor.

One thing has become very clear , no film or post production business in South Africa can rely on the local economy alone anymore. The volume simply is not there. At The Refinery, we made a deliberate choice to diversify beyond South Africa, both creatively and geographically, and 2025 fully tested that decision. Despite the global slowdown, we delivered an unprecedented volume of work across film, television, streaming and advertising this year. That scale was only possible because of long term investment in systems, remote collaboration and senior talent who can operate independently without compromising quality.

One of the most meaningful creative milestones this year was attracting international projects that were not shot in South Africa, yet still chose to bring their post production work here. Projects involving talent like Kevin Costner and Will Smith did not come to us because South Africa is cheap. In fact, with the rand performing stronger than expected, price alone is no longer our biggest advantage. They came because of the calibre of talent, because of taste and trust, and because South African artists and technicians consistently deliver at a global level.

That distinction matters. Racing each other to the bottom on price is not sustainable. Actors, crew, post houses and producers are all feeling the same pressure from different directions. This is the moment to band together, to think globally, and to build businesses and careers that can survive cycles instead of relying on short term booms. 2026 will not be about returning to how things were. It will be about moving forward into something new.
