Business FlySafair Pilot Strike Escalates as Pay Dispute Grounds Flights and Talks Stall

July 21, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

FlySafair is currently dealing with a deepening conflict with its pilots, who have gone on strike over salary demands and working conditions. The airline was recently forced to cancel about 8% of its scheduled flights after some pilots, who had previously confirmed availability, informed the airline at the last minute that they would not be flying.

The pilots are represented by the trade union Solidarity, which announced the strike after rejecting a 5.7% salary increase offered by the airline. Instead, the union is demanding a 10.5% raise on base salaries, as well as additional pay for flights and bonuses. Solidarity says that 90% of its pilot members voted in favour of the strike, indicating strong dissatisfaction within the group. According to the union, pilots are also seeking to restore salaries that were cut during the Covid-19 pandemic and are pushing for changes to working conditions that would allow for more rest and family time.

The situation quickly escalated. While Solidarity initially gave notice of a one-day strike, FlySafair responded by issuing a lockout notice, barring striking pilots from returning to work for seven days, with the possibility of an additional seven-day extension. Solidarity then extended its strike notice to 14 days. The Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration (CCMA) offered to mediate the dispute, but Solidarity claims the airline has refused to participate in further discussions. The union says the one-day strike notice was meant to prompt FlySafair back to the negotiating table but accuses the company of inflaming the situation instead.

FlySafair has said that operations are continuing mostly as scheduled, but that affected customers on canceled flights have been contacted directly. The company emphasized that the pilots who pulled out had previously confirmed their availability, which contributed to the disruptions.

According to FlySafair, its pilots are already among the highest-paid in the country. Captains earn between R1.8 million and R2.3 million a year, with some even earning more than executives at the company. These salaries, the airline says, are benchmarked against local competitors and generally exceed industry norms. In terms of workload, FlySafair reported that its captains flew an average of 63 hours last month, well within the legal limits set by aviation authorities.

Additional responsibilities such as training and standby time are factored into their schedules and often served at home under minimal restrictions. The airline argues that Solidarity’s full set of demands would lead to a 20% increase in costs, which it says is unsustainable for any business. The 5.7% increase currently on the table is 1.5% above the inflation rate and includes bonuses and other benefits. FlySafair maintains that its offer was made in good faith and is intended to balance fair compensation with the company’s financial health and the affordability of its services for customers.

Another major point of tension is the airline’s new pilot rostering system, which was introduced at the start of the year. The system gives pilots their schedules in advance and includes features like preferred leave bidding and an organized method to swap duties. FlySafair says it aligns with global industry standards and has been adopted by other airlines in South Africa. It believes the system improves efficiency and gives pilots flexibility.

However, Solidarity and some pilots argue that the system is too rigid and disrupts their personal lives. The union has warned that unless changes are made, this issue could lead to a separate strike.

FlySafair says it has been in consultation over the new rostering system since March 2024 but that the changes being demanded would undermine the airline’s ability to compete and operate efficiently.

As it stands, both sides are firmly entrenched, and unless one yields or mediation resumes, the dispute could drag on, potentially leading to further flight disruptions and long-term consequences for both the airline and its employees.
