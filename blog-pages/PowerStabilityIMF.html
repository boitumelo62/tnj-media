The International Monetary Fund has significantly improved its outlook for South Africa’s economy after a year that proved more resilient than many had expected. In its January Economic Outlook, the IMF now estimates that South Africa recorded economic growth of 1.3% in 2025 and expects a modest improvement to 1.4% in 2026.

Although this pace of growth remains weak compared with other emerging markets and the broader Sub Saharan region, it marks a clear improvement on earlier projections. At the beginning of 2025, the IMF had forecast growth of 1.5%, but this was revised down sharply to 1.0% by midyear as concerns mounted over the impact of a global tariff war led by the United States. By October, after the damage from tariffs appeared less severe than feared, the projection was nudged up to 1.1%. Now, heading into 2026, the IMF has upgraded its view once again, bringing the estimate back to 1.3%.

This revised forecast matches the World Bank’s own projection for 2025 and reflects several positive developments. A more reliable electricity supply, a strong agricultural season, and improved business confidence toward the end of the year all helped support economic activity. The end of load shedding, in particular, played a critical role. In 2024, persistent power cuts forced the IMF to slash its growth outlook for South Africa from 1.8% to just 0.5%. Actual growth that year came in at 0.6%, meaning the projected 1.3% expansion in 2025 would represent a doubling of economic momentum.

Looking ahead, both the IMF and the World Bank expect growth to edge up to 1.4% in 2026 and 1.5% in 2027. However, these projections are heavily dependent on South Africa maintaining momentum on reforms, particularly in electricity generation, infrastructure development, and transport logistics, while also making the economy more attractive to private investment.

Private consumption and private sector investment are expected to remain the main drivers of growth in 2026. While the prospect of continued improvement is encouraging, it also highlights a sobering reality. Even growth of 1.5% is far too low to meaningfully reduce South Africa’s extremely high unemployment rate or generate strong gains in living standards. With population growth running at around 1.3%, economic expansion at these levels implies that many South Africans are becoming poorer on a per capita basis.

The growth outlook also falls well short of the ambitions set by the government of national unity under President Cyril Ramaphosa. At the start of 2025, the administration expressed confidence that structural reforms could lift growth to around 3% in the medium term. Based on the latest IMF and World Bank forecasts, the economy is likely to undershoot that target by roughly 1.5 percentage points.

Investec’s longer term scenarios suggest that South Africa could reach 3% growth over five years in its base case, but in more optimistic scenarios where the country truly thrives, sustained growth of between 5% and 7% would be required. This broader context makes the IMF and World Bank projections more concerning, as South Africa continues to lag far behind its peers. Sub Saharan Africa is expected to grow by an average of 4.4% in 2025 and 4.5% in 2026, while emerging markets overall are forecast to expand by 4.3% and 4.1% respectively.

In response to the latest projections, the government said it remains committed to deepening reforms, unlocking investment, and building a more resilient and sustainable economy, even as the challenge of closing the growth gap becomes increasingly clear.
