Business South Africa Opens Small Parcel Delivery Market to Competition

December 18, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The Department of Communications and Digital Technologies has officially ended the South African Post Office’s long standing monopoly on the delivery of small parcels. This change was announced in a recent government gazette issued by Minister Solly Malatsi, who amended Schedule 1 of the Postal Services Act under Section 15(3). The amendment removes “small parcels” from the list of services reserved exclusively for the Post Office.

Schedule 1 of the Act defines postal services that only the Post Office is allowed to provide. Until now, this list included letters, postcards, printed matter, small parcels and other postal items that fell within specific size and weight limits. With the removal of small parcels from the schedule, the Post Office no longer has a legal monopoly over this segment of the delivery market.

For years, the Post Office held an exclusivity licence that prevented private couriers from delivering packages weighing under one kilogram or items that fit within certain box or cylinder dimensions. The licence also restricted private operators from issuing postage stamps and providing roadside collection and address boxes. These limitations were a constant source of tension, particularly among private courier and logistics companies, which argued that the monopoly stifled competition and innovation.

In May 2024, the department extended this exclusivity to 1 April 2025. However, the original 25 year exclusivity period granted to the Post Office when the Act came into effect in 2000 was set to expire on that same date regardless. In practice, the monopoly had already become difficult to enforce due to the operational collapse of the Post Office, which struggled to consistently deliver even the services it was meant to protect.

Against this backdrop, Minister Malatsi launched a review of postal legislation earlier this year to assess whether existing laws still serve the Post Office’s mandate. While only one amendment has been made so far, removing small parcels from the reserved services is a major shift that opens the door to full competition in a key part of the delivery market.

This change comes at a critical time for the Post Office. In its strategic plan presented to Parliament, the organisation outlined ambitious growth targets, aiming to increase revenue from about R1.9 billion in 2024 to R5.2 billion by 2029. Central to this plan is capturing a share of the business to business and business to consumer delivery markets, as well as a quarter of the consumer to consumer market within five years. The Post Office also expects new income streams from providing connectivity services in underserved areas and from the rollout of the AARTO system, once it becomes fully operational.

If these plans succeed, the Post Office’s courier and parcel business is projected to grow dramatically, from contributing just R38 million in 2024 to around R1.4 billion by 2029. A key assumption behind this growth was the competitive edge provided by exclusive rights to deliver small parcels, particularly in the fast growing e commerce space. With that advantage now disappearing and the AARTO system facing delays, serious questions remain about how the Post Office will compete effectively in a fully open market.

Despite the significance of the amendment, the Post Office has been given some breathing room. As part of the gazetted changes, the minister extended the exclusivity period for a further year. This means the remaining reserved services will only lose their protected status one year after the publication of the gazette, with exclusivity finally ending on 12 December 2026.
