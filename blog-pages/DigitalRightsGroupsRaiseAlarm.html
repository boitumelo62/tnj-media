Media Digital Rights Groups Raise Alarm Over Online Censorship After Palestine Action Ban

A coalition of digital rights organisations, civil liberties advocates, and legal experts has issued a public warning that the UK’s recent ban on the activist group Palestine Action could lead to sweeping online censorship and the suppression of lawful political speech.

In an open letter addressed to the UK communications regulator Ofcom and major tech companies including Meta, Alphabet, TikTok’s ByteDance, and X (formerly Twitter), the signatories caution that platforms may over-moderate content related to Palestine, activism, or protest for fear of breaching the Online Safety Act.

The warning follows the 5 July designation of Palestine Action as a terrorist organisation under the Terrorism Act 2000. The group, known for staging disruptive protests at facilities linked to arms manufacturer Elbit Systems, is now illegal to support, promote, or fund. Public expressions of "reckless support" are also criminalised, carrying potential prison terms of up to 14 years.

Critics argue that the government has used anti-terror legislation to target non-violent, political activism. Though the group’s actions have caused property damage, they have not involved physical harm.

Since the proscription, police have arrested over 200 people, many on suspicion of showing public support, such as displaying logos, holding placards, or wearing affiliated slogans.

The open letter coordinated by the Open Rights Group and supported by Index on Censorship, legal scholars, and civil society organisations warns that automated moderation systems may interpret any content referencing Palestine Action or related causes as potentially illegal.

“This chilling effect could silence marginalised voices and undermine public discourse,” the letter states.

The signatories call on Ofcom and the tech platforms to:

  • Issue clear, narrow guidance on what constitutes illegal support
  • Avoid algorithmic overreach that penalises lawful discussion
  • Provide independent appeals mechanisms for users whose posts are wrongly removed

The letter comes as Palestine Action co-founder Huda Ammori successfully obtained High Court permission to challenge the government’s ban. A full judicial review will examine whether the proscription was lawfully enacted and whether it violated rights to freedom of expression and assembly.

“To treat peaceful protest as terrorism is deeply dangerous,” said one legal observer. “It sets a precedent where political opposition is treated as a security threat.”

Under the Online Safety Act, tech platforms are obligated to remove illegal content swiftly or risk penalties. Digital rights groups argue this has created a perverse incentive for over-compliance, leading to blanket suppression of controversial topics.

The letter also warns of growing self-censorship, particularly among minority communities and activists who may fear being mistakenly flagged for supporting terrorism.

Among the letter’s more than 1,300 signatories are musicians, academics, trade unionists, and faith leaders including singer Charlotte Church and veteran peace campaigner Angie Zelter.

With the judicial review set to proceed in the coming months, legal experts say the outcome could reshape how protest movements are treated under British law and whether online platforms can truly balance national security with democratic freedoms.

In the meantime, campaigners continue to call on regulators and companies alike to protect the digital public square from undue political interference.
