Business Revenue Shortfall Spells Trouble for 2026

October 1, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The South African Revenue Service (SARS) is falling behind on its goal to collect an extra R35 billion in tax revenue this year, and that’s bad news for taxpayers. While it’s still on track to meet its normal target for 2025/26, it hasn’t brought in enough to hit the higher target that would help ease the country’s growing fiscal pressures. So far, SARS has collected around R39.3 billion more than what’s needed to meet the baseline goal, but still short of the R49.3 billion it should have brought in to reach that extra stretch target.

The gap in collections is important because the government was counting on that money to avoid introducing more taxes. Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana has already warned that if revenue doesn’t improve, spending cuts could be on the table and that taxpayers should brace for an additional R20 billion in taxes in 2026. Earlier this year, Godongwana tried to plug the hole by increasing VAT to 17%, but that plan fell flat. After political backlash and a court challenge, he had to settle for a final budget with no VAT increase at all, but that also meant no easy way to raise the extra revenue.

Treasury turned to other sources instead they hiked the fuel levy and froze inflation related adjustments to tax brackets and medical aid credits. That means taxpayers are now paying more by default, without seeing a formal tax increase. This all comes at a time when many believe South Africans are already overtaxed. Economists argue the country has passed the Laffer Curve peak the point at which increasing taxes starts to bring in less revenue because people stop working, investing, or complying. SARS has been tightening the screws, ramping up audits and going after sectors more aggressively.

Adding to the frustration is the fact that South Africa’s government isn’t spending its money effectively. The country’s fiscal multiplier essentially a measure of how much economic growth is generated from government spending is below one. That means every rand the government spends results in less than one rand of growth. For taxpayers, it feels like throwing money into a hole. And despite poor performance and low economic growth, government spending keeps rising. There are plans to ramp up social grants.

Many South Africans already have to pay for private healthcare, security, education, and even their own power supply because the state can’t provide these basics reliably. In return for their taxes, they’re getting less and less while being asked to give more and more. Godongwana is expected to present the country’s medium term budget policy in November. Whether that plan includes more taxes or deeper spending cuts remains to be seen, but one thing is clear, the gap between what government expects from taxpayers and what it gives back is widening.
