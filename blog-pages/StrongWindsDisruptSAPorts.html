Business Strong Winds Disrupt South African Ports as Businesses Face Delays and Stock Shortages

December 18, 2025 ● Tebuho Zongwana

Strong winds battering several of South Africa’s major ports are causing significant shipping delays, leaving businesses across the country struggling with low stock levels and disrupted supply chains. Port authorities have confirmed that adverse weather conditions have forced restrictions on vessel movements, slowing both imports and exports at a critical time for trade.

The affected ports include key gateways for consumer goods, fuel, and industrial materials. High winds have made it unsafe for large vessels to dock or depart, while loading and offloading operations have also been intermittently suspended. As a result, ships have been forced to anchor offshore for extended periods, creating a backlog that could take weeks to clear.

Businesses reliant on just in time deliveries have been particularly hard hit. Retailers report delays in receiving essential goods, while manufacturers warn that shortages of raw materials could disrupt production schedules. Some companies have already begun rationing stock, while others are exploring more costly alternatives such as air freight to meet urgent demand.

Industry analysts say the situation highlights the vulnerability of South Africa’s logistics network to weather related disruptions. While strong winds are not unusual during certain seasons, the scale of the current delays has raised concerns about contingency planning and infrastructure resilience. Logistics firms have called for improved coordination between port authorities, shipping lines, and transport operators to minimise the knock on effects of such disruptions.

Consumers may also begin to feel the impact, with the possibility of higher prices and limited availability of certain products if delays persist. Economists warn that prolonged port congestion could place additional pressure on inflation and economic growth, especially if export shipments are held up and foreign buyers turn to alternative suppliers.

Port authorities have indicated that operations will resume at full capacity once weather conditions improve. In the meantime, they are working to prioritise critical cargo and manage the growing backlog. Government departments responsible for transport and trade are monitoring the situation closely, given the importance of ports to the national economy.

As businesses wait for relief from the weather, the disruption serves as a reminder of how external factors can quickly ripple through supply chains, affecting companies, workers, and consumers alike.
