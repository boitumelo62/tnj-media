Business Equal Pay and Openness at the Core of New Fair Pay Bill

July 24, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The newly introduced Fair Pay Bill could bring major changes to how salaries and hiring are handled across South Africa. Presented to Parliament in June 2025, the bill seeks to amend the Employment Equity Act of 1998 with the aim of promoting transparency, reducing inequality, and improving fairness in the labour market. Legal experts from Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr Mkefa Yvonne, Sashin Naidoo, and Thato Makoaba believe this bill has the potential to create a more just and open workplace culture.

One of the bill’s central proposals is to stop employers from asking job applicants about their previous salaries. Instead, employers would be required to state a fixed salary or salary range when advertising a job, classifying a position, hiring someone, or transferring staff. The only exception to this rule is if an offer has already been made and the applicant voluntarily requests that their past pay be taken into account.

Another key provision targets pay secrecy. Although current labour law already gives workers the right to discuss their pay, many employment contracts still include clauses that discourage it. The Fair Pay Bill would go further by outlawing these confidentiality clauses, ensuring that employees can speak freely about their earnings without risking disciplinary action.

These changes are meant to align South Africa with global efforts to close wage gaps and address pay disparities, especially those based on gender or race. Transparent pay structures also help attract candidates who are better suited to a role, saving time and reducing the risk of unfair labour practices or discrimination claims. In turn, this could boost trust within companies, leading to better employee retention and a more engaged workforce.

However, the bill isn’t without its challenges. Some industries such as those with freelance or newly emerging roles may struggle to set standard pay ranges. Smaller businesses with limited resources might find it difficult to meet the bill’s requirements, such as updating recruitment procedures or investing in HR training. The bill does offer some flexibility, like allowing broad salary bands instead of fixed figures, but concerns remain about how public salary information could affect competition.

For employees, the benefits are clear. Job seekers would have more power during salary negotiations and wouldn’t be limited by their previous earnings. They’d also know what kind of compensation to expect before applying making the process more transparent and efficient. The right to openly discuss pay would be firmly protected.

As the bill moves through the legislative process, it will undergo legal reviews and be opened up for public comment before facing deeper scrutiny by the Portfolio Committee on Employment and Labour. If passed, it will require employers to overhaul their pay and hiring practices. Companies would need to train staff, revise application forms, and make use of compensation tools and market data to ensure fair and justifiable salary decisions.

Ultimately, the Fair Pay Bill represents a bold step toward creating a labour market where employees are paid fairly and can speak about their earnings without fear a shift that could bring lasting change to South Africa’s workplaces.
