Media Rethinking Cultural Relevance

September 22, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The term "cultural relevance" gets thrown around a lot in marketing circles, but we need to take a closer look at what it actually means. When a brand quickly jumps on a trending meme, mentions a viral sports moment, or offers a surface level nod to whatever’s hot online that week, it might seem like they’re being relevant but are they really? This kind of engagement often feels shallow. It’s like showing up just long enough to snap a selfie and then disappearing again. It doesn’t reflect a real understanding of the culture or the people within it.

It’s more like digital tourism than true participation. What’s often missing is the deeper understanding of why cultural relevance actually matters and more importantly, how to make it meaningful and lasting. Real cultural relevance doesn’t come from chasing trends. It comes from recognizing and connecting to the bigger forces shaping how people think, live, and relate to each other. It’s about tapping into the underlying needs, desires, tensions, and values that define a moment not just reacting to it.

Brands should be asking deeper, more useful questions. What are our customers really dealing with? What do they care about? What are they hopeful for or afraid of? And how does our brand’s purpose align with those things in a way that actually helps or matters? The real shift here is going from reacting to the noise to focusing on what’s real for your audience. And that’s a much stronger foundation to build on.

You can see this in the way some brands approach culture. Nando’s in South Africa is a great example. They’ve built a reputation for consistently showing up in moments that matter, often using sharp, witty commentary on politics and current events. What makes their presence work isn’t just the humour it’s the fact that it comes from a brand voice they’ve developed over years. When Nando’s says something about a big issue, it doesn’t feel like a stunt it feels like part of an ongoing conversation.

Nike is another strong example, but on a global scale. Their most powerful messaging often reaches far beyond athletics. Think of the “Dream Crazy” campaign with Colin Kaepernick. It wasn’t just a celebrity endorsement it was a statement, one that tied into deeper cultural conversations about race, protest, and purpose. Because Nike’s brand identity has always been about resilience and courage, the campaign didn’t feel like a gimmick it felt authentic.

Brands that connect with people on a cultural and emotional level are more trusted, and that trust can translate into stronger loyalty and even a willingness to pay more. In a world where people are increasingly good at ignoring ads, relevance is what makes someone stop, pay attention, and care. And that’s not something competitors can easily copy.

That said, getting cultural relevance wrong can do real damage. When brands make token gestures especially around sensitive issues people notice. And thanks to social media, those mistakes can go viral in the worst way. Audiences today are more attuned to authenticity than ever. If something feels insincere or opportunistic, it’s called out almost instantly.
