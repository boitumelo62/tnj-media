Business Tshwane Shuts Down Bronkhorstspruit Shoprite Over Missing Trading Permits

October 28, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The City of Tshwane forced the Shoprite store in Bronkhorstspruit to close last week, on 22 October, after discovering that it was operating without valid trading permits. This action is part of the municipality’s Clean Governance and Law Enforcement Campaign, which focuses on restoring order, safety, and accountability in how businesses operate across the city.

Mayor Nasiphi Moya personally led the operation and made it clear that the city will no longer tolerate businesses that ignore its laws. She emphasized that no brand, no matter how big, is above the law, and that every business must comply with Tshwane’s regulations if they want to trade there. During the same inspection, officials also found that a KFC outlet in the area was operating without an updated trading license.

The city said the Shoprite Group could face further penalties for allowing one of its franchise outlets to operate without the proper permits. Mayor Moya explained that these enforcement efforts are part of a broader plan to make sure all businesses both big and small operate legally and responsibly. She said the city is determined to build a law abiding environment where businesses respect local by laws and standards.

In recent months, Tshwane has been stepping up its enforcement operations across key business areas, including Hammanskraal and the Pretoria CBD. The Bronkhorstspruit shutdown is one of several similar operations planned under the Clean Governance initiative. Since the start of the year, Moya has joined multiple by law enforcement raids across Pretoria, where dozens of businesses have been closed for operating without proper permits.

One major operation in August targeted the Marabastad retail market, where inspectors found that many traders lacked the necessary trading licenses. Working with the Department of Home Affairs and the South African Police Service, officials inspected 43 businesses. Some were issued compliance notices, two were disconnected from illegal electricity connections, and authorities closed seven shops and 20 stalls immediately.

Moya said the closure of the Marabastad market was based on city by laws that require all traders to hold valid permits. She reported that in August alone, the city inspected 341 premises. Of these, 175 were compliant, while 166 failed to meet regulations. As a result, 135 statutory notices and 29 fines amounting to R125,000 were issued.

While the mayor insists that these actions are necessary to uphold the law and ensure fair business practices, not everyone agrees with the city’s approach. Critics argue that sudden closures, often without prior warning, have a severe impact on employees and small business owners who depend on daily trade to survive.

Earlier this year, a business owner in Mamelodi even took the city to court after their store was closed for alleged compliance issues. The court ruled that Tshwane could not arbitrarily shut down legally operating businesses and stressed that law enforcement must always be fair and within legal boundaries.

Following that ruling, the Democratic Alliance (DA) criticized the city’s heavy handed methods. The party said that while businesses should follow regulations, enforcement should be done in a way that protects jobs and supports the local economy rather than harming it. The DA argued that harsh closures only push more South Africans into poverty and unemployment. Instead, it called for a more balanced and supportive approach one that helps traders become compliant through clear guidelines, permit assistance, and support mechanisms rather than punishing them outright.

In response to the Bronkhorstspruit closure, Shoprite told BusinessTech that it had not received any prohibition of trade notice from regulators who visited the store and therefore believed the closure was unjustified.
