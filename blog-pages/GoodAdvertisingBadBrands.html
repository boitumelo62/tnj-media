Media Good Advertising Is Dangerous for Bad Brands

January 14, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

More than sixty years ago, in Confessions of an Advertising Man, David Ogilvy wrote the line that should still be pinned above every marketer’s desk: “The consumer is not a moron; she is your wife.” It was a reminder that people are perceptive, rational, and perfectly capable of seeing through nonsense.

It is only in a kind of Trumpian dystopia that constant repetition, no matter how irrational or untrue, can be mistaken for persuasion. In the real world, the old advertising truism still applies: nothing will destroy a bad brand faster than good advertising. If what you promise loudly and repeatedly is not matched by reality, you are simply accelerating disappointment.

This tension is at the heart of the recently published WARC Marketer’s Toolkit 2026, which identifies the growing gap between brand promise and customer experience as a major threat to marketing effectiveness. That gap is widening at the same time as customer service and product quality are being steadily eroded by short term thinking. The now widely used term “enshittification” captures this slow deterioration perfectly. What brands say they stand for, and what customers actually experience, are drifting further apart.

WARC’s findings make it clear this is not some abstract problem playing out in the metaverse. It is happening in everyday life. Managing brand experience and creating clear, positive points of user experience differentiation has become increasingly difficult, and global consumer dissatisfaction suggests many brands are failing to meet even basic expectations. According to WARC, 32 percent of customers will abandon a brand they love after just one bad customer experience, based on PwC data from 2023. Forrester reported in 2024 that 39 percent of brands saw significant declines in brand equity as a direct result of poor customer experience.

At the same time, we have convinced ourselves that flooding people with exposure is the same thing as giving them a reason to care. From Ogilvy’s perspective, this is exactly what it means to treat consumers like morons. The practice has become so commoditised that we no longer even talk about “advertising.” Everything is just “content.” We hand out licences freely to produce it, calling people influencers or content creators, and hope that volume will somehow make up for a lack of substance.

After a decade long race to the bottom, where success has too often been defined by cheap reach and discounted impressions, it is at least encouraging to see more serious thinking about the relationship between creative quality and media investment. For too long, procurement driven measures of return on media investment have equated quantity with value. Research from the World Federation of Advertisers’ Project Spring in 2020 showed that 92 percent of marketing and procurement leaders believed the way marketing procurement is perceived inside their organisations could be improved.

Looking back over nearly fifty years in advertising and media, I have come to believe that the real starting point is not about fine tuning the balance between content and media exposure. It is not primarily about attention metrics or contextual relevance, important though those are. The real shift begins with accepting a more fundamental truth advertising and media are not ends in themselves. They are subsets of marketing.

Any serious media campaign should begin by allocating a meaningful portion of the budget to measurement. Without understanding the baseline and without committing to tracking outcomes, all the content in the world is just noise.
