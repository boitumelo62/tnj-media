Media How a Fearless Voice Lost Its Spirit

September 3, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The Mail & Guardian was born in 1985 during the darkest days of apartheid, originally named the Weekly Mail. It wasn’t just another newspaper it was a defiant act of resistance, created by journalists like Anton Harber and Irwin Manoim who refused to stay silent under a regime that censored the press, jailed reporters, and banned dissent. From the start, it was meant to challenge power, not to be profitable. Readers didn’t just buy it for news; they bought it to stand with truth, to support a voice that fearlessly exposed injustice.

Despite constant threats, lawsuits, and raids, the paper persisted. Every edition carried the fire of resistance, a relentless commitment to accountability. It helped define what journalism could and should be in South Africa unflinching, principled, and brave. When apartheid fell and South Africa entered a democratic era, the Weekly Mail had to reinvent itself. In 1995, it became the Mail & Guardian, entering into a partnership with the UK’s Guardian Media Group. This wasn’t just a name change; it was an evolution, positioning the paper as a serious institution for the continent still investigative, still bold.

Through the late 1990s and early 2000s, the Mail & Guardian stood at the top of its game. It exposed scandals in government and business, trained some of South Africa’s finest journalists, and published stories that could shake Parliament. More than its scoops, it stood for something. Buying it was a statement of values of truth, integrity, and fearless journalism.

But in 2002, a turning point arrived when Zimbabwean publisher Trevor Ncube bought a controlling stake. At first celebrated as African ownership of an African publication, the move slowly shifted the paper’s identity. In Zimbabwe, journalism often meant survival and caution. That mindset seeped into the newsroom. Reporters began to feel boundaries where none had existed before. The edge dulled. The energy shifted. The paper started pulling punches.

The investigative fire didn’t disappear overnight, but it flickered. Readers noticed. What was once essential became optional. Then came the digital storm: the collapse of print revenue and the rise of free online content. Where others leaned into their core values, the Mail & Guardian was already weakened. By the 2010s, the decline was impossible to ignore staff cuts, shrinking impact, and financial strain.

Its fall wasn’t just about disrupted business models; it was about lost character. Once that spirit was gone, everything else followed. Ncube hadn’t just bought a paper he inherited a tradition of fierce journalism. Instead of protecting it, he reshaped it through his own political experience. The result was the slow extinguishing of a vital voice.

What remains today is memory. The memory of a paper that once inspired, challenged, and held power accountable. The Mail & Guardian deserves to be remembered not only for its rise, but also for its decline a reminder that institutions can only survive if they remain true to their character. Once that is traded away, decline is inevitable.
