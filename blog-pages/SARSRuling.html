Business Court Backs SARS in R46 Million Tax Avoidance Case

October 24, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The South African Revenue Service (SARS) has won another major court battle, strengthening its campaign to crack down on taxpayers who try to dodge their tax responsibilities. In this latest case, SARS used the General Anti Avoidance Rules (GAAR) against a taxpayer who had been routing their income through foreign companies to avoid paying tax. For years, this person had been using these offshore structures to generate Secondary Tax on Companies (STC) credits and disguise their income as tax free dividends instead of taxable earnings.

When SARS uncovered the scheme, it disagreed with how the income was being classified and applied GAAR to reclassify the payments as ordinary income. The Tax Court sided with SARS, agreeing that the main purpose of the taxpayer’s setup was to gain a tax advantage and that, if not for this arrangement, a significant amount of income tax would have been owed. SARS ultimately recovered R46 million from the taxpayer. According to tax experts at Tax Consulting SA, this ruling is another sign of SARS tightening its grip on tax evasion and closing loopholes in the system. Earlier this year, SARS also won other major cases, reclaiming R40 million from invalid tax rebates and R30 million from a taxpayer who used creative accounting to lend money to their own companies.

The experts said the most recent decision highlights SARS’ determination to challenge schemes designed to exploit the law for tax benefits. Under sections 80A to 80L of the Income Tax Act, SARS has the power to disregard or reclassify any arrangement that leads to a tax benefit but lacks real commercial substance. In this case, the court found that the taxpayer’s structure was only meant to disguise income and had no genuine business purpose. The court also reaffirmed that what matters is substance over form. Just because payments were labelled as “dividends” did not mean SARS couldn’t treat them as ordinary income when the real nature of the payments suggested otherwise.

This approach shows that SARS and the courts are more concerned with economic reality than clever structuring. Interestingly, SARS’ use of GAAR is still being tested in other cases, including one involving Absa that has reached the Constitutional Court. While that judgment is still pending, the latest ruling shows that the courts are willing to back SARS when it uses GAAR to dismantle tax driven schemes that lack commercial justification. The decisive factor in this case was the “but for” test meaning that if not for the artificial structure, the taxpayer would have had to pay normal income tax.

Tax experts say this case confirms that SARS is moving from a reactive approach to a more proactive and strategic one. It is now using its legal powers to prevent tax avoidance before it happens, rather than just responding afterward. This should be a clear warning to companies and individuals who rely on creative accounting or complicated structures to reduce their tax burden.

SARS has made it clear that any arrangement primarily aimed at avoiding tax even if it technically follows the law is now a target. This includes executive pay setups, cross border arrangements, corporate restructures, and financing deals that focus more on tax savings than on real economic activity. Simply put, if a transaction is driven by tax motives rather than genuine business reasons, it’s at risk. SARS has shown that layering entities or adding complexity will no longer protect taxpayers from scrutiny.
