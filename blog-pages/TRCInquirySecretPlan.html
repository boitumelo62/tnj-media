Media TRC Inquiry Hears Claims of Secret Plan to Block Apartheid Prosecutions

South Africa’s long running struggle to address crimes committed during apartheid has come under renewed scrutiny after testimony at an inquiry led by retired Constitutional Court justice Sisi Khampepe.

The inquiry, which is examining whether political interference stalled prosecutions linked to apartheid era crimes, heard allegations that a secret political strategy dating back to 2003 sought to halt such cases through legislative means. The claims suggest that efforts may have been made within government to prevent the National Prosecuting Authority from pursuing cases recommended by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

The TRC, established in the 1990s after the end of apartheid, was tasked with uncovering human rights abuses committed during the previous regime. While many perpetrators received amnesty in exchange for full disclosure, others who failed to apply or were denied amnesty were meant to face prosecution. However, many of those cases never reached court, leading to years of frustration from victims’ families and human rights groups.

During proceedings this week, testimony indicated that discussions within political circles may have focused on creating legal barriers to stop these prosecutions from moving forward. The inquiry is investigating whether senior officials interfered with the justice system and whether decisions were made to effectively shelve cases involving apartheid era crimes.

Families of victims have long argued that justice has been delayed for decades. Advocacy groups have also accused successive governments of failing to prioritise the prosecution of unresolved cases despite recommendations from the TRC.

The inquiry chaired by Khampepe is expected to examine documentary evidence and hear from several former officials involved in government and the prosecuting authority during the early 2000s. Its findings could shed light on why many of the cases identified by the TRC have remained unresolved for so long.
