Business Designated employers urged to comply with new employment equity requirements

January 13, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

Understanding the Employment Equity Amendment Act 2025: Key Changes and Compliance Strategies

The Department of Employment and Labour has warned designated employers that they have until midnight on Thursday, January 15, 2026, to submit their annual Employment Equity (EE) reports. This deadline is significant as it marks the first reporting cycle since the amendments to the Employment Equity Act came into effect. Under the amended law, designated employers are now required to prepare and implement Employment Equity Plans covering the five year period from September 1, 2025, to August 31, 2030.

The amended Act mainly applies to businesses with 50 or more employees and is aimed at ensuring that workplaces better reflect South Africa’s demographics at all occupational levels. To support this goal, the department has introduced numerical targets for 18 different industries. These targets focus on increasing the representation of designated employees, which include Black people (African, Coloured and Indian), women, and persons with disabilities.

Employers who fail to submit their EE reports face serious consequences. They risk fines of up to R1.5 million or 2% of their annual turnover, whichever is higher. In addition, they may lose their Employment Equity Compliance Certificates, which are required for companies that want to do business with the government or any state entity.

The Employment Equity Amendment Act, No. 4 of 2022, officially came into operation on January 1, 2025, and since then two sets of regulations have been published. These regulations are intended to guide both employers and employees on how to interpret and apply the amended law, including how to meet sector specific employment equity targets. Under the Act, designated employers will not be judged only against national targets, but also against their own annual goals that contribute towards the relevant five year sectoral targets.

According to the department, the amendments are designed to give the Minister of Employment and Labour, Nomakhosazana Meth, the authority to set sector specific numerical targets to ensure fair representation of suitably qualified people from designated groups. The changes also bring Section 53 of the Act into full operation, making an EE Compliance Certificate a requirement for accessing state contracts. At the same time, the amendments aim to reduce the regulatory burden on small businesses that employ between one and 49 people.

The department has reiterated that the purpose of the Employment Equity Act is to promote equal opportunity and fair treatment in the workplace by eliminating unfair discrimination and implementing affirmative action measures. However, reactions to the new laws have been divided. Some see them as a necessary step to transform a deeply unequal labour market, while others argue that they amount to discrimination. The Black Economic Empowerment Chamber has welcomed the changes, saying the new regulations and sector specific targets will accelerate transformation in the workforce. Frik Boonzaaier from the chamber has pointed out that the flexibility built into the system gives employers room to adapt gradually over the coming years.

On the other hand, the Democratic Alliance, South Africa’s second largest political party, has challenged the constitutionality of the amendments. In 2025, the party launched a legal case against Section 15A of the Act, arguing that it represents an abuse of state power. The DA has said the section violates the Constitution’s guarantee of equality before the law and its prohibition of unfair discrimination, arguing that forcing employers to hire or exclude people based on race is unconstitutional. The case was heard in the Pretoria High Court in May 2025, and a judgment is still awaited.
