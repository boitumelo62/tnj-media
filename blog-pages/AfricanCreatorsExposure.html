Media Why African Creators Deserve More Than Exposure

November 10, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Popular Blogs:

Media

Designing Africa’s Media Future

Tech News

Empowering Creatives Through AI Literacy

African creators are shaping global culture in powerful ways, but too often, they do so without ownership, recognition, or fair pay. From Burna Boy’s landmark 2021 neighbouring rights deal with Kobalt to Nollywood filmmakers turning to YouTube after streaming platforms reduced African commissions, there are signs of progress yet they sit alongside deep structural challenges. At the 2025 Met Gala, designers like Thebe Magugu and Anifa Mvuemba showcased Africa’s creativity to the world, but millions of African TikTok creators still earn next to nothing despite their massive online influence. It’s a painful irony: Africa produces culture the world craves, but its creators rarely control it.

Creative sovereignty goes beyond just making money. It’s about protecting the dignity and originality of African ideas. Economic rights give creators the power to earn from their work, while moral rights allow them to claim authorship and safeguard the integrity of their creations. Although most African countries have ratified global agreements like the Berne Convention, outdated systems, high costs, and slow bureaucracies make it hard for creators to enforce these rights. Many first time or informal creators remain locked out of full ownership simply because they don’t know how to navigate intellectual property processes. Without knowledge and access, even existing laws offer little protection.

The loss isn’t just cultural it’s economic. When African creativity leaves the continent without fair compensation, both money and cultural power drain away. Global platforms and distributors often offer “exposure” instead of real pay, but exposure doesn’t cover living expenses. Despite Afrobeats being streamed over 13 billion times on Spotify in 2022, many artists still struggle financially. Local challenges like high subscription fees, currency conversion issues, and limited monetisation options make it even harder for African artists to benefit from global success.

When creators own their rights, however, the benefits ripple outward. Local ownership reinvests wealth into studios, mentorships, and cultural programmes that strengthen industries and empower communities. Nollywood, now the world’s second largest film industry, generates over $1 billion annually and employs more than a million people, while South Africa’s creative sector contributed R161 billion to the economy in 2020. Yet, without stronger IP protections and infrastructure, this potential remains underdeveloped.

Cultural appropriation adds another layer to the problem. South African designer Laduma Ngxokolo accused Zara of copying Xhosa-inspired knitwear in 2018, and the story of Solomon Linda’s Zulu song “Mbube”which became the global hit “The Lion Sleeps Tonight” with little benefit to his family remains a painful reminder of how often African creativity is repackaged for profit elsewhere. Global pop culture continues to borrow African aesthetics, from music to fashion, without credit or investment in their origins. Africa’s influence is celebrated worldwide, but African creators are too often left out of the rooms where decisions and profits are made.

On platforms like TikTok, the problem takes a digital form. With over 140 million users across Africa, the app is a hub of creativity, but its monetisation systems largely exclude the continent. The Creator Fund and similar programmes operate in the US, Europe, and parts of Asia, meaning African creators depend on inconsistent brand deals to make a living. Viral fame brings visibility but not income, leaving creators as unpaid engines of global trends. Until platforms localise monetisation and include African markets, this imbalance will persist.

Ultimately, creative sovereignty is not a side issue it’s central to Africa’s future. If the continent’s creativity continues to be exported without ownership, it risks losing both its wealth and its voice. But with fair systems and transparent structures, African creators can build economies that thrive on local innovation and global respect. They don’t need permission to lead only the means to own what they create. The time to build those means is now.
