Business Rand Strengthens, but Godongwana Cautious on Chances of Credit Upgrade

November 14, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa would certainly welcome an upgrade to its credit rating, but Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana isn’t expecting it. He says the bias that ratings companies show toward African countries is real, so he’s not assuming anything ahead of S&P Global Ratings’ latest review. Speaking in Cape Town a day after delivering a budget that boosted investor confidence, Godongwana said an upgrade would be a welcome surprise rather than something he’s counting on.

S&P currently rates South Africa’s long term foreign debt at BB minus, which is three steps below investment grade, though it does have a positive outlook. The agency is set to issue its updated view on Friday. The minister’s budget lifted optimism by introducing a formal 3% inflation target, reporting stronger than expected revenue collection, and sticking to the promise that South Africa’s debt to GDP ratio will peak this year before starting to fall. Markets reacted quickly: the rand strengthened to below 17 per dollar for the first time since early 2023, and yields on 10 year government bonds eased to around 8.6% from 8.8% before the announcement.

Several private economists believe the budget gives S&P enough reason to upgrade the country, and Godongwana agrees that the current rating doesn’t match South Africa’s recent progress. Moody’s and Fitch also keep South Africa below investment grade, and he argues they’re all behind the curve. He noted that differences in how debt is measured might be one of the few sticking points. Godongwana also expressed frustration shared by many African finance ministers, that rating agencies consistently judge African nations more harshly than others with similar financial conditions. This bias, he said, raises borrowing costs unfairly.

Countries like Ghana and Zambia, which struggled with debt partly because of the pandemic, have been especially critical of how quickly they were downgraded. According to Godongwana, there’s data to back up the claim that African countries receive weaker ratings even when their fiscal numbers match those of better rated countries. He added that African nations generally repay their debt even under severe hardship. The budget also reaffirmed the government’s plan to finalize a fiscal anchor next year essentially a framework to help ensure responsible financial management.

While South Africa already targets a primary budget surplus, Godongwana is cautious about locking the government into rigid numerical rules that could limit policy flexibility. Instead, he prefers a broader framework that outlines principles rather than strict targets, giving Parliament oversight without letting technocrats override elected leaders. He believes such an approach could help the country’s new coalition government remain stable. Earlier this year, disagreements over a proposed tax increase delayed the 2025 budget for months, putting the coalition under strain.
