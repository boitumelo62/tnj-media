Business Repo Rate Held at 6.75 Percent as Reserve Bank Signals Cautious Economic Stance

February 2, 2026 ● Tebuho Zongwana

The South African Reserve Bank has decided to keep the repo rate unchanged at 6.75 percent, with Governor Lesetja Kganyago confirming that the prime lending rate will remain steady at 10.25 percent. The decision reflects a continued cautious approach as policymakers weigh inflation risks, global uncertainty, and domestic economic pressures.

By holding the rate, the Bank signals that while inflation may be moderating, it is not yet comfortable enough to justify a cut. The repo rate is the benchmark interest rate at which the central bank lends to commercial banks. When it remains unchanged, borrowing costs across the economy tend to stay stable, affecting home loans, vehicle finance, and business credit.

In recent months, inflation trends and currency volatility have remained key concerns for monetary authorities. A stable rate suggests that the Bank wants more consistent evidence that price growth is under control before shifting policy. It also points to concern about external risks such as global market movements and commodity price fluctuations, which can quickly affect the local economy.

For consumers, the decision brings short term predictability. Bond holders and other borrowers will not see immediate changes in their monthly repayments. However, relief in the form of lower rates is also delayed, which may disappoint households already under pressure from living costs.

Businesses and investors often view a rate hold as a sign of policy discipline. It provides planning certainty, especially for sectors sensitive to financing costs such as property, construction, and manufacturing. At the same time, slower rate easing can temper expectations for rapid economic expansion.

The Reserve Bank has repeatedly stressed that its primary mandate is price stability. Governor Kganyago indicated that future decisions will depend on incoming data, including inflation readings, growth indicators, and global developments. Markets will now look ahead to the next policy meeting for signals on when a rate adjustment may become more likely.
