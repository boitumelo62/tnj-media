Business South Africa’s sugar sector faces structural strain from tax and trade dynamics

January 14, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa’s sugar industry, one of the country’s biggest agricultural employers, is under serious threat, with industry leaders warning that government tax policy could push it into long term decline. SA Canegrowers says urgent intervention is needed to prevent widespread job losses, damage to rural communities and growing risks to food security.

The sugar sector supports more than one million livelihoods across the country, directly and indirectly, with most of these jobs concentrated in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga. About 27,000 small scale growers and 1,100 large scale farmers form the backbone of the industry, many of whom have been under sustained pressure for more than a year. Rising input costs, volatile global prices and weak local demand have already strained growers, and SA Canegrowers argues that the continued application of the sugar tax is making an already difficult situation worse.

At the same time, the country has seen a sharp rise in sugar imports. Data analysed by SA Canegrowers from SARS shows that more than 153,000 tonnes of sugar were imported between January and September 2025. This is a dramatic increase compared with just under 21,000 tonnes during the same period in 2020, and far higher than the previous peak of just over 55,000 tonnes recorded in 2024. The organisation says this surge has nothing to do with a shortage of local sugar, but is instead driven by distortions in global markets.

SA Canegrowers chairman Higgins Mdluli says much of the sugar entering South Africa is heavily subsidised in the countries where it is produced. He argues that while importers benefit from short term profits by selling this sugar at local prices, local growers are left at a disadvantage. Globally, the sugar market is oversupplied, with major producers offloading excess stock at extremely low prices. Subsidies, favourable exchange rates and sluggish growth in global demand all add to the problem.

In this environment, Mdluli believes South Africa needs strong protection for its domestic sugar market. Without it, local growers are forced to compete with dumped imports while also dealing with policies that reduce demand for their product. He warns that replacing locally produced sugar with imports undermines food security, weakens rural economies and threatens the future of a strategic agricultural industry.

South African growers already produce enough sugar to meet domestic demand, meaning imported sugar does not fill a supply gap. Instead, it replaces local sugar on supermarket shelves and in supply contracts with food and beverage manufacturers. According to SA Canegrowers, this reduces the volumes processed by local mills and weakens the entire value chain, from farms through to factories.

The organisation is also sharply critical of the sugar tax, arguing that it continues to punish local producers and manufacturers without clear evidence of health benefits. While SA Canegrowers supports efforts to improve public health, it says there is no solid proof that the tax has led to meaningful improvements in health outcomes. What is clear, it argues, is the economic damage. When the tax was introduced in 2018, the sugar industry lost more than 16,000 jobs in its first year.

Mdluli has described the sugar tax as an unproven policy experiment with very real consequences for rural jobs and investment. He says future decisions should be guided by reliable health data and an accurate understanding of sugar consumption in South Africa, while also taking into account the impact on employment, economic activity and local food production.
