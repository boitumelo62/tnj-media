Media The New Era of Radio Marketing

September 16, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Radio marketing today sits at the crossroads of creativity, business, psychology, and technology. It’s no longer just about broadcasting audio it’s about connecting people through multiple platforms, and building a relationship between listeners, content, and brands. As listening habits shift toward digital spaces like apps, websites, and music platforms, the way broadcasters engage their audiences has had to evolve too. Radio still thrives on locality. Regional and community stations succeed because they reflect the people, events, and culture of the areas they serve.

National stations, however, have a trickier task staying relevant across different provinces, languages, and cultures. It’s not easy to create a shared experience when one event might be accessible in Joburg but out of reach for listeners in Limpopo. Content often reflects where the station is based, leaving others feeling left out. To get a better sense of how national radio brands handle this, I spoke with three SABC marketing managers based in Auckland Park, Melba Thompson from Metro FM, Busi Phakathi from 5FM, and Amoré Swanepoel from RSG. Each brings a unique background and perspective, but they all agree that radio marketing today goes far beyond just radio.

For Thompson, Metro FM’s personalities are the heart of the brand. Her job is to package that magic the voices, the music, the vibe in ways that attract audiences and appeal to advertisers. For Phakathi, 5FM isn’t just a station, it is Youth culture. Her job is to make sure the brand lives where young people do: on their phones, at school events, in their everyday conversations. Swanepoel’s challenge is different she’s helping RSG, a heritage Afrikaans brand, stay culturally relevant for new, younger listeners without alienating its loyal base. One common theme is that programming and marketing can’t work in silos. Content and campaigns must speak the same language. Phakathi says the two need to move together, so what people hear on air matches what they see and experience off air.

Thompson describes marketing at Metro FM as an amplifier not creating content, but enhancing and spotlighting what’s already there. Swanepoel is slowly shifting RSG’s tone to attract new listeners without losing the trust of its long-time audience. Finding new listeners while keeping the old ones is the eternal radio balancing act. Swanepoel experiments with podcasts and small digital campaigns for younger users. Phakathi goes directly into schools, embedding 5FM in the lives of teens. Thompson relies on events and activations to invite new listeners into Metro FM’s world, all while keeping loyal fans engaged. Marketing nationally comes with its own challenges. You can’t be everywhere, and you can't speak to everyone the same way.

Thompson sees this as a creative opportunity weaving in regional flavour while keeping the Metro FM thread consistent. Phakathi uses schools as national touchpoints. Swanepoel picks her battles, partnering with local events instead of trying to spread thin across the country. When it comes to serving listeners versus serving advertisers, the answers vary. Phakathi firmly believes the listener comes first build a strong connection and the advertisers will follow. Swanepoel and Thompson see both sides as equally important and believe the real goal is building bridges between marketing and sales.

Budget is always a challenge. None of them has the luxury of big campaigns. Swanepoel makes clever use of partnerships and reuses content online. Phakathi leans into social media for reach and engagement. Thompson focuses on in-person brand experiences that create a lasting impression without massive spend. Technology, especially AI, is beginning to shift the way campaigns are created. Thompson has already tried AI-generated campaigns and saw big wins in cost and speed. Phakathi is excited about how AI can personalise marketing for youth, while Swanepoel remains cautious, especially when it comes to language and authenticity something RSG takes seriously. They also see influencer marketing differently. Phakathi and Swanepoel are skeptical young audiences can spot a paid endorsement a mile away.

Thompson prefers using the station’s own personalities, who already have built-in trust with the audience. As for PR, it’s not dead, but it’s changed. It now needs to be interactive, visual, and connected to experiences that audiences care about. Each manager has their favourite campaign. For Phakathi, it was 5FM’s Live Loud which spoke directly to the youth. Swanepoel lit up when she spoke about RSG’s Bakgat campaign, which celebrated language and community. Thompson believes in using strong content and bold events to let people experience the brand for themselves.

In the end, great marketing is about authenticity, connection, and relevance. For Phakathi, it means making the audience the hero. Thompson values creativity, consistency, and meaning. Swanepoel believes simplicity and cultural relevance are key. Radio may be changing, but when marketing is done right whether through a live event, a podcast, or a tweet it still moves people, builds community, and stays with you long after the sound is gone.
