Business Africa’s Streaming Boom Is Coming

August 19, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Turning PR and Digital Marketing into a Growth Engine

As global subscription growth begins to slow, streaming platforms are turning their eyes to Africa. And for good reason. The continent is mobile first, home to a young and digitally curious population, with a strong creative culture that’s eager to be seen and heard. But success here isn’t about duplicating what worked elsewhere it’s about adapting, rethinking, and, above all, listening.

Traditional TV still matters across many African countries. It’s familiar, free to air, and doesn’t rely on data or high speed internet. For many households, especially where digital infrastructure lags or data is expensive, TV remains the easiest way to access entertainment. But younger viewers the ones growing up online are increasingly drawn to streaming, where they can choose what to watch and when. Streaming offers flexibility and scale. It fits into the way people already use their phones: on the move, switching between Wi-Fi and mobile data, consuming bite sized content in the language they speak at home.

In South Africa alone, over 8 million people are now using paid or subscription streaming platforms, and that number is expected to keep growing steadily. But for that growth to continue, platforms need to meet people where they are. At Viu, for example, what works isn’t just having content it’s having the right content, at the right price, in the right format. Low cost daily bundles, mobile optimized design, and local language options matter. These choices speak to the reality of users who don’t always want or need a monthly subscription they want flexibility, relevance, and value.

TV may still be easier to access no log ins, no data costs, no waiting for a stream to buffer. Streaming, on the other hand, comes with challenges, high data prices, patchy connectivity, and income inequality that limits who can afford regular access. Even in relatively advanced markets, these hurdles mean people might dip in and out of streaming platforms depending on what’s on offer or what they can afford that month. And that’s okay the goal isn’t constant retention, but reduced friction and real value. Partnering with telecoms is a gamechanger.

Zero rated content and bundled deals make streaming more accessible, especially for price- sensitive users. But it doesn’t stop there platforms also need to stop chasing Western models that don’t fit the African market. Relying too heavily on international content or premium only subscriptions risks leaving out the very people who could become loyal users if only the content reflected their lives. This is where ad supported video on demand (AVOD) becomes a powerful strategy. It’s not a fallback it’s a way to scale, especially for audiences without credit cards or stable income. AVOD brings in new users, and it opens the door for advertisers to reach engaged, mobile first viewers who care about brands and notice when ads speak their language literally and culturally. Campaigns that feature local languages and relatable narratives resonate far more than one-size-fits-all messaging.

Blending AVOD and SVOD letting users switch between free and premium access is working in emerging markets. It keeps users engaged, gives them options, and respects the economic realities many face. But more than anything, success hinges on content that feels like it belongs. Local content isn’t a nice to have it’s the key. Africa is full of stories waiting to be told. The world has already embraced African music, and video is next but only if the industry invests. That means not just funding one off productions, but supporting the entire ecosystem: writers, directors, translators, marketers. It’s about jobs, identity, and long term value creation.

Too many platforms still treat Africa as one big market but the continent is anything but. It’s made up of 54 different countries, each with unique languages, cultures, and economies. What works in Nigeria may not work in South Africa. A strategy built for Nairobi might fail in Accra. Assumptions and shortcuts will cost companies their audience. Underinvestment is another issue. The audience is there, and they’re ready they have smartphones, improving internet access, and a clear appetite for content that reflects who they are. What’s missing, often, is the commitment to build long term, locally relevant offerings rather than just dipping a toe in.

Africa doesn’t need to “catch up” to the rest of the world in streaming. It’s building something new mobile first, grounded in culture, and shaped by access and authenticity. The future of video here isn’t just about high tech platforms or massive libraries of content. It’s about understanding people’s lives, speaking their languages, and showing up with purpose. The platforms that win in Africa won’t be the biggest or the flashiest. They’ll be the ones that respect the audience, invest in local talent, and build for the realities on the ground not just the metrics on a dashboard.
