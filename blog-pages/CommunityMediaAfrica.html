Media Community Media in Africa Is Evolving

August 21, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Community media in Africa is going through a major shift. It’s no longer just about small, local newspapers or radio stations reporting on nearby events. Instead, it’s turning into something broader and more dynamic often digital, often mission-driven, and always adapting. Traditionally, community media was thought of as content created by and for a particular group, usually tied to a specific place.

But that definition has never been set in stone. In reality, the label has been used for a mix of grassroots organisations and local outlets owned by big media companies. Some of them are deeply connected to their communities, while others have used the “community” label more as a branding tool than a true reflection of their work. That’s sparked debate about what actually counts as community media. A recent survey by CivicSignal, a media research programme from Code for Africa, looked at 42 community media organisations across 11 African countries and found that things are changing fast. One major shift is in the kind of content being produced.

In the past, these outlets tried to cover a bit of everything local crime, events, announcements, politics. But now, many are narrowing their focus. They’re choosing topics that matter deeply to their audiences climate change, gender justice, youth empowerment, public health and building their identity around those themes. Take Bus Radio in Kenya. It’s run by young people and focuses on climate change. They use local languages, music, and storytelling to explain environmental issues in ways that connect with everyday life. In Nigeria, Naija Feminists Media publishes content daily on women’s rights through platforms like WhatsApp and Instagram.

These outlets aren’t just delivering news; they’re creating space for conversations and community building around shared struggles and goals. At the same time, while these organisations may be leaning into niche topics, they’re not completely abandoning traditional news. Many still report on general interest stories, but they’re doing so in a more deliberate way less reactive, more strategic. They’re picking stories that fit with their values and their audience’s needs. Another huge change is in how the news is being delivered.

The old model radio transmitters, printing presses, hand-delivered papers is expensive and harder to sustain. Meanwhile, people, especially young people, are getting their news on mobile phones and social media. So outlets are adapting. Some are combining FM radio with digital tools like livestreams, podcasts, and WhatsApp broadcasts. In rural areas, radio is still powerful, but in cities, online platforms are taking over.

Still, there’s a challenge. Even though people are getting more news online, they don’t always trust it. According to the 2025 Digital News Report, many users in countries like Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa say they struggle to tell what’s real and what’s fake. Others are just tired of the constant flow of bad news and tune out altogether. Here’s where community media has a unique edge. Because they’re close to their audiences sometimes literally they’re better positioned to build trust. Their content feels more relevant and more credible, especially when it’s made in collaboration with the community itself.

That spirit of participation is still central. Community media has always been about people telling their own stories, not just consuming what someone else decides to report. But now, that participatory approach is extending beyond just content into the business side of things. Outlets are sharing tools and resources to survive. They’re experimenting with new ways to make money running events, offering memberships, creating sponsored content, or building a presence on social media that supports revenue. Still, challenges remain, especially around digital skills and the ability to grow sustainably.

So what is community media becoming? It’s changing, no question. It’s becoming more focused, more digital, and more in tune with identity and lived experience rather than just geography. It’s harder to define, more fluid, shaped by context. It’s wrestling with tough questions about independence, funding, innovation, and inclusion but it’s also finding new paths forward. In the end, community media in Africa isn’t dying it’s evolving. And in doing so, it’s finding new ways to serve, connect, and empower the people it speaks to.
