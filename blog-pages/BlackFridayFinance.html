Business Financial Experts Urge Discipline as Black Friday Fever Grips South Africa

November 5, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Black Friday is fast approaching, and retailers across South Africa are flooding consumers with advertisements promising massive discounts and “unmissable deals.” This annual shopping frenzy has become a highlight on the retail calendar, but experts warn that it also brings financial risks that can easily derail household budgets. According to Salem Nyati, a consumer financial specialist at Momentum Group, the excitement of Black Friday often leads to impulse buying and overspending, which can have lasting effects on financial stability.

Nyati explains that Black Friday isn’t just a sales event it’s a sophisticated exercise in consumer psychology designed to trigger emotions like excitement and urgency. It’s meant to make people act fast and spend without thinking carefully about whether they can afford to. The smartest approach, she says, is to treat Black Friday as just another ordinary day in your financial year. It’s not a reason to throw responsible money habits out the window.

Recent data from PayInc, Africa’s leading automated clearing house, shows how powerful the event has become. In 2024, shopping activity on Black Friday was stronger than a typical day and even slightly higher than in 2023. Shoppers started hunting for deals as early as midnight, with transaction volumes up by 75% compared to the previous Thursday. However, the frenzy was still 11% lower than in 2023, possibly showing a bit more caution from consumers.

Still, with South Africa’s average household food basket now costing around R5,380 as of August 2025 and more than 60% of households struggling to afford basic essentials, many families simply can’t afford to spend recklessly. Nyati warns that retailers use powerful psychological tactics to encourage spending. One is the “discount effect,” where shoppers feel a stronger emotional reaction to missing out on a deal than the pleasure of saving money. Another is the “fear of missing out,” where limited time offers push people to buy quickly. The third is “scarcity marketing,” which uses phrases like “only a few left” to create a false sense of urgency.

Lee Naik, CEO of TransUnion Africa, adds that a 45% increase in personal loan inquiries between October and November 2024 shows that many South Africans are actually borrowing to take part in Black Friday sales. While some of this may suggest more thoughtful use of credit, it also highlights how stretched consumers are. He warns that many households don’t have enough savings to handle financial shocks, and this makes it vital to focus on building up a financial cushion rather than chasing temporary discounts.

Nyati agrees, saying that the true win for consumers isn’t in finding the lowest prices it’s in maintaining financial stability while shopping wisely. The best deal, she says, is one that fits your budget, fulfills a real need, and supports your long-term financial goals. The timing of Black Friday makes things even trickier. It lands right before the costly December holiday season and the stressful “Janu worry” period when school expenses pile up, putting extra pressure on household finances.

Given South Africa’s current economic climate with a high cost of living, stubborn unemployment, and easy access to credit Nyati says thoughtful spending is more important than ever. She emphasizes that the goal isn’t to avoid Black Friday completely, but to approach it with awareness and discipline. Consumers should make choices based on their financial realities, not marketing hype. In a country where financial resilience is critical, a responsible approach to spending during these shopping events can determine whether you end the year in a stable position or start the next one burdened by debt.

Nyati concludes that while Black Friday can offer some real savings, those savings shouldn’t come at the expense of your financial health. As the 2025 shopping season approaches, she urges South Africans to let wisdom guide their decisions instead of impulse. In the end, your future self and your bank account will be grateful that you chose financial security over momentary excitement.
