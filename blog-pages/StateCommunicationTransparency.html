Media Calls Grow for Transparency in State Communication Spending

November 4, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

One issue that keeps coming up whenever people talk about the sustainability of community media is how the government spends money on communication. Every year, state entities spend millions to communicate with the public. There has been a long standing promise from government to allocate 30% of these communication budgets to local and community media. Yet, operators in the sector say that this promise is rarely, if ever, fulfilled.

A recent report by the Media Development and Diversity Agency (MDDA) highlighted this problem. One respondent in the study said they had no idea how many years it had been since the government failed to enforce the 30% allocation from the Government Communication and Information System (GCIS). Without action at the national level, the respondent added, sustainability plans are useless because they cannot be implemented properly. The report, released in late 2024, proposed a sustainability model for community media where one of the key elements was for government to actually follow through on the 30% commitment.

Even though there’s wide agreement that this issue matters, very few people really know how government communication budgets are being spent. There’s little transparency or clarity about where the money goes, and this creates potential risks, as another report, Levelling the Media Playing Fields: Lessons from South Africa argues. The figures that are available aren’t broken down in a way that helps people understand spending patterns.

GCIS, however, isn’t the only player involved. While it’s a major source of funding, other state bodies such as government departments, municipalities, Eskom, and water boards also have their own communication budgets, which they manage independently. This decentralised system can sometimes be beneficial, but it also creates a lot of uncertainty. A community radio manager explained that their relationship with the provincial premier’s office often changed depending on who held the position.

Another broadcaster said that while one of the provinces their station covered regularly bought airtime for public interest campaigns, the other province almost never did. Despite these inconsistencies, government advertising remains a lifeline for local and community media. Many publishers and broadcasters say that official advertising is essential to their survival. In fact, when researchers examined several local newspapers, they found that government ads took up more space than private sector ads in most of them.

This included large, glossy inserts from the South African National Roads Agency, which at the time was facing public criticism over the e-toll system. However, this heavy reliance on government funding also makes community media vulnerable to political pressure. One publisher from the North West province told how a local mayor tried to buy his small newspaper. When the publisher refused, the mayor started a rival paper and then ensured the original one received no municipal advertising. The paper eventually shut down, only reopening later when local politics changed.

In Mpumalanga, two newspapers in nearby towns have chosen opposite strategies one tries to stay on good terms with local authorities to secure advertising, while the other avoids government ads completely, believing its critical reporting would make it impossible to get them anyway. Community media groups in Argentina are now campaigning to strengthen these rules and improve transparency. In South Africa, by contrast, the problem is barely being discussed.
