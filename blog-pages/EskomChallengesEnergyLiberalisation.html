Eskom Restructuring Aims to Open South Africa’s Energy Market

Eskom’s recent legal challenge against the National Energy Regulator of South Africa (NERSA) is more than just a court case it’s a blatant attempt to block long-overdue reforms in the country’s electricity sector. On 24 July 2025, Eskom approached the High Court in Pretoria, claiming that NERSA’s decision to grant five new electricity trading licences amounts to a sudden and unvetted change in energy policy.

Eskom says this move could “upend” the entire electricity system in South Africa. But that claim ignores history and facts. The idea of a competitive electricity market isn’t new it’s been part of national policy for over two decades, and Eskom has been part of that conversation the whole time.

Back in 1998, the White Paper on Energy Policy laid the groundwork for opening up South Africa’s electricity sector. It specifically called for competition in electricity supply and a move away from a single, centralised utility model. That meant letting other companies not just Eskom sell electricity. It also meant making the national grid accessible to everyone on fair terms.

This reform path was reinforced in 2019 through the Department of Public Enterprises’ “Roadmap for Eskom in a Reformed Electricity Supply Industry.” That roadmap envisioned Eskom being unbundled into three separate businesses generation, transmission, and distribution and made it clear that electricity traders would be part of the new market.

According to the roadmap, traders would be able to sell power directly to customers, while paying regulated fees to use Eskom’s grid. So this wasn’t a surprise; it was a step forward in a long, deliberate process.

Yet, Eskom now claims these new licences are part of a “unilateral” policy shift that bypassed public input. That simply isn’t true. NERSA followed all the required legal procedures before issuing these licences, including a public consultation process in which Eskom itself took part.

The legal framework that allowed for these licences the Electricity Regulation Amendment Act came into force at the start of 2025, after years of open debate and legislative review. Eskom had every opportunity to challenge it then, but didn’t. Now, after the licences have been issued, Eskom suddenly wants to stop the process it once supported. That looks like a deliberate strategy to delay progress and protect its monopoly.

What’s worse is the language Eskom uses in its court papers. It claims that traders will “poach” its best customers, leaving Eskom stuck with expensive social obligations. That’s misleading. Even if a customer switches to a new trader, they still use Eskom’s grid and pay for it through regulated fees. Eskom still earns revenue from that.

The idea that competition will hurt the poor is also disingenuous. Eskom argues that if it loses customers, it won’t be able to fund subsidies for poorer households. But let’s be honest Eskom has already been failing to meet those responsibilities. Many poor communities face constant power cuts, high tariffs, or are disconnected entirely. The real threat to affordability and access isn’t competition it’s Eskom’s own inefficiency, outdated systems, and inability to modernise.

It’s also important to remember that electricity trading isn’t some radical new experiment. South Africa’s first trader, PowerX, was licensed back in 2009. Several others have followed. Eskom never raised any legal objections until now. This sudden resistance suggests not a genuine concern for public interest, but fear of losing market share to more agile, customer-focused alternatives.

In fact, Eskom’s own logic contradicts itself. On one hand, it claims competition will hurt its finances and limit its ability to help the poor. On the other, it’s consistently failed to deliver on those very promises. The electricity sector is evolving Eskom helped shape that evolution, but now refuses to participate in it.

If Eskom’s court case succeeds, the consequences will be serious. It would send a message to investors that South Africa’s energy policies are unstable and can be reversed when powerful interests are threatened. It would undermine trust in regulators like NERSA and stall the development of a more reliable, affordable, and competitive electricity system.

Ultimately, this legal battle is not just about trading licences. It’s about whether South Africa chooses progress over protectionism. Eskom is trying to rewrite history, misrepresent the law, and use social justice as a shield for its own failures. The public and the courts must not fall for it. South Africa’s energy future can’t be held hostage by the past.
