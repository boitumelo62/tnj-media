Business BAT Factory Closure Threatens Thousands of Jobs as Illicit Cigarette Trade Surges

British American Tobacco’s decision to halt cigarette production in South Africa is expected to place more than 35,000 jobs at risk, dealing a major blow to tobacco farmers and communities that have depended on the company for more than a century.

BAT has confirmed it will mothball its Heidelberg manufacturing plant at the end of the year and shift to an import-based supply model. While the company insists it is not exiting the South African market, the closure of its only local factory has raised serious concerns about employment and the future of the legal tobacco industry.

Limpopo Tobacco Processors, which contracts about 100 commercial and small-scale farmers across Limpopo the North West and Mpumalanga, supplies most of its crop to BAT South Africa. Director Francois van der Merwe said BAT previously bought up to 90 percent of locally produced tobacco, but that figure has fallen to about 60 percent as illicit cigarettes increasingly dominate the market.

Van der Merwe said the industry’s decline accelerated after the Covid 19 tobacco sales ban, which was later ruled unlawful. He said illegal cigarette traders filled the gap and legal manufacturers never recovered, despite smoking levels remaining high.

BAT has committed to continue buying leaf tobacco from local farmers, offering some relief, but industry leaders warn this is not a long-term solution unless government acts decisively against illicit trade.

BAT executives say the legal industry has been shrinking for more than a decade. In 2014 legal manufacturers supplied most of the cigarette market, but criminal syndicates now control an estimated 75 percent of sales. Excise revenue has dropped sharply despite higher taxes, while overall cigarette consumption has increased due to illegal products.

An Oxford Economics report shows the Heidelberg plant supports nearly 4,000 jobs in the Lesedi district and more than 31,000 jobs nationally through its supply chain. The closure is expected to have a severe ripple effect on families and small businesses, with many workers supporting multiple dependants.

BAT has warned that unless illicit trade is brought under control, the remaining legal tobacco industry, including farmers, could collapse entirely.
