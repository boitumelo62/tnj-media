Business Policy failures blamed for BAT shutdown and thousands of jobs at risk

January 19, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

The closure of British American Tobacco’s only cigarette manufacturing plant in South Africa is being described as a self inflicted jobs crisis, driven by weak policy decisions and failures in enforcement. Business Leadership South Africa chief executive Busi Mavuso said BAT’s announcement on 15 January highlights how legitimate manufacturing has steadily been pushed out of the country. The factory has been running at just 35 percent of its capacity and is no longer viable, leading to the loss of 230 direct jobs. The consequences extend far beyond the plant itself, with as many as 35,000 livelihoods across the value chain expected to be affected, including farmers, distributors and retailers.

Mavuso said the situation has been years in the making and is largely the result of the rapid growth of illicit trade. Illegal cigarettes now make up as much as 75 percent of the tobacco market, compared with about 33 percent before COVID-19. She explained that the sharp increase followed policy mistakes during the pandemic, when the sale of legal cigarettes and alcohol was banned, a move that was later ruled unconstitutional. At the same time, enforcement capacity was diverted elsewhere, allowing illegal networks to build distribution systems that continued operating even after restrictions were lifted.

These failures were compounded by steep increases in excise duties on legal products, which widened the price gap between legal and illegal cigarettes. Criminal operators took advantage of this, selling illicit cigarette boxes for less than the R26.22 that should be paid in excise and VAT alone. These products bypass health warnings and age restrictions and cost the state an estimated R30 billion a year in lost tax revenue.

Despite the damage already done during the pandemic, Mavuso warned that further policy changes could make matters worse. Parliament is currently considering the Control of Tobacco Products and Electronic Delivery Systems Bill, which introduces new regulations intended to discourage smoking. However, researchers, experts and industry players, including BAT, have raised concerns that some of the proposals could unintentionally hand the market entirely to criminal networks. Measures such as plain packaging, critics argue, would make it even easier for illicit traders to dominate.

Mavuso stressed that the problem extends far beyond tobacco. She said the same policy and enforcement failures are undermining legitimate businesses across many sectors. Illicit alcohol sales, for example, have grown by 55 percent in volume since 2017, with one in five drinks now coming from illegal sources. Counterfeit medicines, fake branded clothing, electronics, cosmetics and even food products are also increasingly common. The Consumer Goods Council of South Africa estimates that the illicit economy now accounts for around 10 percent of GDP, underscoring the scale of the challenge.

According to Mavuso, industry groups have put forward practical solutions, but many of these proposals have been sitting with government departments for years without action. She said urgent intervention is needed on several fronts. SARS requires dedicated resources for its illicit trade task team, including skilled investigators and modern technology to monitor supply chains. The police need specialist units capable of building cases against sophisticated criminal networks rather than focusing only on street level traders. The National Prosecuting Authority must prioritise cases that target the organisers and financiers behind these operations.
