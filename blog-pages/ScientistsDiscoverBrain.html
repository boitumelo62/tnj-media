Media Scientists Discover Brain-Like Communication System in the Human Gut

A team of scientists from Duke NUS Medical School and Nanyang Technological University (NTU) has uncovered a previously unknown communication system in the human gut that resembles how neurons interact in the brain.

Published in Developmental Cell, the study reveals that specialized support cells in the intestine, called telocytes, use long, finger-like extensions to deliver signals directly to stem cells responsible for maintaining the intestinal lining. This discovery challenges the long-held belief that these signals, particularly Wnt proteins, simply diffuse through surrounding tissue.

“This is not a passive process,” said Professor David Virshup of Duke NUS. “We now know that the delivery of Wnt signals is highly targeted. It is more like a direct message than a broadcast.”

Using advanced imaging techniques in mouse models, researchers observed that telocytes extend branched projections known as cytonemes into the intestinal crypts, which are tiny pockets housing stem cells. These cytonemes establish physical contact with individual stem cells, forming what appear to be communication junctions similar to neural synapses.

Assistant Professor Alexander Ludwig from NTU described the findings as a significant shift in how scientists understand cell communication in the gut. “It is a level of precision we did not think existed outside the nervous system,” he said.

The intestinal lining is among the body’s most rapidly renewing tissues, and its upkeep relies on the accurate coordination of stem cells. The discovery that this process may be governed by direct, brain-like signaling mechanisms opens new possibilities for treating diseases such as colon cancer and inflammatory bowel disease.

Lead author Dr Gediminas Greicius emphasized the surprise behind the results. “We were not expecting to find this level of organization,” he said. “Sometimes you study something simple and end up uncovering something profound.”

The discovery not only deepens understanding of intestinal biology but also highlights the gut’s complex and sophisticated internal network. It may be far more similar to the brain than previously imagined.
