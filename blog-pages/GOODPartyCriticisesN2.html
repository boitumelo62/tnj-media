Media GOOD Party criticises proposed N2 wall near Cape Town airport

February 13, 2026 ● Tebuho Zongwana

The GOOD Party in the Western Cape has criticised plans to build a wall along a section of the N2 near Cape Town International Airport, arguing that the project reflects apartheid style spatial planning and risks deepening inequality in the city.

The proposed wall, which is intended to improve safety and security along the busy highway, has sparked debate over whether such infrastructure measures address underlying social challenges or simply create physical barriers between communities.

GOOD Party leaders said the plan sends the wrong message at a time when the city should be working towards integration and inclusive urban development. They argue that physical walls have historically been used to separate communities, and that similar approaches in the present day undermine efforts to build a more cohesive and equitable city.

“The idea of erecting walls to manage social problems is deeply troubling,” the party said in a statement. “It reinforces the notion that certain communities must be hidden or excluded rather than supported and developed.”

Supporters of the wall argue that it is a practical response to safety concerns, including pedestrian accidents, crime and disruptions to traffic along the N2 corridor. Authorities have previously cited the need to protect motorists and pedestrians, as well as to prevent people from crossing the highway in dangerous areas.

Urban planning experts have noted that infrastructure projects often become politically charged in South Africa due to the country’s history of segregation and spatial inequality. They caution that while safety measures are important, they should be paired with broader social interventions such as improved housing, public transport access and community development.

The City of Cape Town has not yet provided full details on the final design and implementation timeline for the wall. However, the criticism from GOOD is likely to intensify public debate over how cities balance security, mobility and social justice.

As discussions continue, the issue highlights the ongoing tension between urban safety measures and the broader goal of dismantling the physical and social divides inherited from the past.
