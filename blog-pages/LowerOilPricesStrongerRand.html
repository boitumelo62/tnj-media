Media Lower oil prices and stronger rand bring fuel relief for SA drivers

January 13, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

South African motorists are enjoying some relief at the pumps at the moment, thanks to a combination of lower global oil prices and a stronger rand. Petrol prices dropped at the start of 2026, and another sizeable decrease is expected in February. Early figures from the Central Energy Fund suggest that both petrol and diesel are currently over recovering, meaning prices could still come down further. Petrol is showing an over recovery of about R1.20 per litre, while diesel is even higher at around R1.75 per litre.

Energy expert Roland Tatnall says fuel prices are mainly influenced by two things the international price of crude oil and the rand’s exchange rate against the US dollar. Right now, both are moving in South Africa’s favour. Crude oil prices have fallen sharply over the past year, while the rand has strengthened against the dollar. Tatnall notes that Brent crude is trading at around $60 a barrel, down from roughly $80 at the start of 2025. This decline over recent months has fed directly into lower fuel prices locally.

As long as these two factors continue to move together, Tatnall believes fuel prices should remain fairly stable. However, he warns that this situation is delicate. If either oil prices or the exchange rate shifts, fuel prices will respond quickly. One of the biggest risks is the rand itself, which is known for being volatile. Even if oil prices stay low, a weaker rand could easily push fuel prices higher.

There are also questions about how long oil prices can remain at current levels. Tatnall points out that prices between $56 and $60 a barrel are quite low and may not be sustainable in the long run. He also mentions political pressure in the United States to keep oil prices down, noting that President Donald Trump has spoken about wanting prices closer to $50 a barrel. While that might sound like good news for consumers, Tatnall doubts it is realistic. Much of US shale oil production becomes uneconomical at those levels, as new wells typically need prices between $60 and $65 a barrel to break even.

Looking beyond short term price movements, Tatnall says global oil demand is slowly changing, partly because of the rise of electric vehicles. This trend has already had an impact, particularly in China, where electrification has reduced oil demand by about a million barrels a day. In countries like Norway, almost all new cars sold are electric. Even so, Tatnall does not believe oil demand will collapse anytime soon. With global consumption still around 100 million barrels a day, he expects the oil industry to remain relevant for years to come.

While current fuel prices are lower than they have been in a long time, Tatnall cautions motorists not to assume this is the new normal. Any movement in the rand or oil prices could quickly change the picture. For now, prices may stay relatively steady if conditions hold, but South Africans should be prepared for possible volatility ahead.
