Business Kganyago Says Rand Doesn’t Reflect SA’s True Economic Value

October 16, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South African Reserve Bank Governor Lesetja Kganyago recently pointed out that the rand is extremely undervalued. According to him, various studies even simple comparisons like the Big Mac Index show that the rand should be much stronger, possibly around R7 to the dollar, not the R18+ range it’s currently sitting in. He explained this while speaking at the Drakensberg Inclusive Growth Forum, hosted by the Kgalema Motlanthe Foundation.

Kganyago reflected on how the rand has steadily weakened over the past 20 years. And while traditional economic theory, like the Mundell Fleming model, suggests a weaker currency should help boost growth through more competitive exports, that hasn't happened here. The rand has lost value, but the economy hasn’t improved in fact, it's also weakened. Back in the 2000s, people used to argue that the rand was too strong and too unstable. Today, it's much weaker and also less volatile, but this hasn’t helped the country grow the way it was expected to.

When you use purchasing power parity basically measuring how much the rand can buy in South Africa versus what a dollar can buy in the US the picture becomes clearer. Something as basic as the Big Mac Index shows that the rand should be 50–60% stronger. More advanced measures from the World Bank support this view too, suggesting the fair exchange rate is around R7.4 to the dollar. Yet last year it sat at around R18.3. Kganyago compared the currency to a company’s share price it reflects confidence. When things are going well in a country, investors are more confident, and the currency gains value.

But when there are problems, like state capture or rising debt, investors pull out and the currency weakens. So it’s not just about how much money is moving in or out it's about how the world views South Africa’s overall direction. Good governance, reform, and stable finances make investors more confident. Bad news does the opposite. He also touched on how trade plays a role. Even though a weak currency should help exports, it’s not that simple. Many South African exporters still rely on imported components to make their goods, and investment often requires importing machinery or technology.

In many emerging markets, businesses actually invest more when their currencies are stronger because it’s cheaper to import what they need. So there’s value in maintaining a balanced exchange rate not too weak, not overly strong. South Africa has let the rand float freely for the past 27 years. Kganyago suggested it may be time to rethink how the currency is managed, especially as the country shifts toward a future of lower inflation. The Reserve Bank has been pushing to bring inflation closer to 3%, even though the official target range is currently between 3% and 6%, with a focus on the 4.5% midpoint.

There was some early disagreement between the Reserve Bank and the Finance Minister over this, but they’re now working together to tighten the range, since both sides agree that the current one is too broad. Kganyago made it clear that aiming for high inflation is risky it pushes prices up faster than in other countries and ultimately hurts competitiveness. That’s why the Reserve Bank is working on anchoring inflation at a lower and more stable level, to support long term growth and bring back investor confidence hopefully helping the rand to find its fair value again.
