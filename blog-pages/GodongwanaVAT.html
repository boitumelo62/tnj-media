Business Pressure Mounts on Godongwana Over VAT and Small Business Support

November 12, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana is set to deliver the Medium Term Budget Policy Statement (MTBPS) on Wednesday, 12 November. This year’s speech comes after delays and heated debate over the government’s proposal to increase the Value-Added Tax (VAT) rate. Many South Africans, especially small businesses, have voiced concern about the impact of higher taxes during tough economic times.

One of the strongest voices calling for change is Lula, a company that supports small and medium enterprises (SMEs). Lula wants the government to show fiscal discipline but also offer targeted support to small businesses. SMEs make up around 40% of South Africa’s GDP, yet many of them struggle to grow because of high operating costs, unstable power supply, and heavy compliance burdens.

Garth Rossiter, Lula’s Chief Risk Officer, said the biggest threat to small businesses isn’t their lack of resilience but rather the government’s slow pace in fixing key issues like electricity, logistics, and debt management. According to him, small businesses cannot confidently invest or hire new workers when the country lacks a clear economic direction.

Rossiter highlighted three main areas that need attention: improving infrastructure, modernising digital systems for procurement and compliance, and introducing meaningful tax relief. He urged the Minister not to increase broad based taxes such as VAT or the fuel levy beyond inflation, as these measures would hurt both consumers and small businesses.

Lula also wants the government to raise the VAT registration threshold, which has been fixed at R1 million for 16 years. They argue that this outdated limit creates unnecessary red tape and costs for small businesses. Rossiter suggested that increasing the threshold to R3 million would ease administrative pressure and allow entrepreneurs to reinvest in their businesses, creating more jobs and growth opportunities.

He added that the upcoming budget should give the private sector confidence that the government is serious about tackling the obstacles that hold back economic activity. Promises made in the budget, he said, must lead to real, visible improvements such as more stable electricity, faster logistics, and simpler compliance requirements.

However, there are growing signs that a VAT rate hike might still be on the way. Economist Dawie Roodt from Efficient Wealth believes that an increase above the current 15% rate is becoming more likely. He pointed out that the government urgently needs more revenue, and since most other taxes are already at their limits, VAT is the only major option left.

Roodt explained that cutting spending would be politically risky for the ruling ANC, making a VAT increase almost unavoidable despite public opposition. He warned that while VAT has gradually risen over the years, such “temporary” tax hikes often become permanent.

He also noted that corporate and personal income taxes are already too high, leaving little room for further increases. South Africa, he said, has reached the point where higher taxes could actually reduce revenue a situation described by the Laffer Curve, which shows that excessive taxation eventually drives people to avoid or escape taxes altogether.

In short, while small businesses are pleading for relief and reform, the government faces growing pressure to raise more money to keep the country running. The Finance Minister’s speech will need to strike a delicate balance between fiscal responsibility and economic growth a task that will shape South Africa’s financial direction in the months ahead.
