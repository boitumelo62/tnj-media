Business SA Pushes to Stop Devastating US Export Tariffs

July 25, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa is just a week away from facing a 30% tariff on exports to the United States — a move that could have serious consequences for several key industries in the country. President Cyril Ramaphosa has acknowledged the threat and said the government is in active talks with the US in hopes of avoiding the worst.

Speaking to the press, Ramaphosa explained that these tariffs could have a heavy impact on sectors like automotive manufacturing, agriculture, and mining. These industries are already feeling the strain, and if the tariffs go into effect, the pressure will only grow. The US is a major trading partner for South Africa, accounting for about 8% of the country’s total exports in 2024, worth roughly R157 billion. A big chunk of that — more than R34 billion — comes from the automotive sector, which is already battling existing trade hurdles and uncertainty around South Africa’s eligibility for AGOA (the African Growth and Opportunity Act).

The warning signs have been there for months. Back in April, former US President Donald Trump announced the tariffs as part of his “Liberation Day” speech, originally setting the start date for April 9. But he paused them for 90 days to allow room for negotiations. That pause is about to end, and the clock is ticking fast.

In May, Ramaphosa led a high-level delegation to Washington to meet with the Trump administration. The goal was to open the door for deeper engagement. Since then, the Department of Trade and Industry has submitted a fresh package of proposals to the US, though some reports have hinted that the US side has gone quiet. Ramaphosa insists that discussions are ongoing, saying both countries are still exchanging ideas and proposals and that the government remains hopeful a solution can be found in time.

But he also admitted that time is running out. While negotiations continue, the government is also working on a backup plan in case the tariffs go ahead. Business leaders are urging the state to be proactive. Busi Mavuso, CEO of Business Leadership South Africa, suggested that support measures similar to those used during the COVID-19 crisis — like relief funds and emergency loan schemes — be reintroduced to help affected companies. She also called for a specific fund to soften the blow of the tariffs and help industries adapt.

There’s also growing recognition that South Africa can’t afford to rely so heavily on a single market. Ramaphosa emphasized the need to find new places to sell South African goods, both within Africa and beyond. He pointed to Africa’s growing free trade potential and mentioned ongoing efforts to tap into major markets like India and China, especially for agriculture and mining.

Ramaphosa said government officials are actively traveling and meeting with partners around the world to boost trade opportunities. “Every time you see us getting on a plane, it’s because we are out there looking for markets — markets that will help us create jobs and grow the economy,” he said. Whether or not the US tariffs can be avoided, South Africa is preparing for impact and beginning to chart a new path forward.
