Media Outa and DA Demand Tax Reform for Taxi Industry

September 24, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa’s taxi industry brings in an estimated R90 billion to R100 billion in revenue every year, yet it contributes almost nothing in taxes. In March 2025, Wayne Duvenage, CEO of the Organisation Undoing Tax Abuse (Outa), pointed out that this entire sector operates outside of the country’s formal tax system. He stressed that bringing the taxi industry into the tax net isn’t about punishing it, but about recognising it as a legitimate and profitable business sector that should be contributing its fair share to the country’s revenue.

Duvenage's view aligns with earlier concerns raised by the Democratic Alliance (DA), which, back in 2021, revealed that only around R5 million in tax is collected from the entire minibus taxi industry. This amount is staggeringly low when compared to the billions the industry earns. According to the DA, most operators report no business income and don’t pay corporate taxes or payroll taxes for their drivers, which is both unfair and illegal under South Africa’s tax laws. Failing to declare income and dodging tax payments is a crime, and the Tax Administration Amendment Act allows for up to two years in prison for such offenses. Despite the legal framework, the South African Revenue Service (SARS) has a tough job enforcing tax compliance in this space.

Most taxi operators deal in cash, and there's very little formal documentation of income or expenses, making it nearly impossible to track earnings accurately or calculate what’s owed to SARS. However, the taxi industry, represented by the South African National Taxi Council (SANTACO), has pushed back against these claims. SANTACO argues that it’s wrong to say the industry doesn't pay tax at all. According to their spokesperson, Mmatshikhidi Rebecca Phala, most operators are sole proprietors and pay personal income tax rather than corporate tax. She also pointed out that many taxi owners have tax clearance certificates from SARS, which they need for things like renewing operating licenses.

In her view, critics fail to understand the informal and individualistic nature of the industry. Even so, Phala admitted that South Africa’s tax system doesn’t fully accommodate how the taxi industry works. Because it remains largely informal, a big portion of the sector still falls outside the broader tax system for businesses. She suggested that the country’s tax laws need to be updated to better integrate the taxi industry and close the current loopholes. While the taxi industry plays a massive role in the economy and provides crucial transport for millions, its contribution to the country’s tax revenue is shockingly small. There’s growing pressure on SARS and the government to formalise this sector and ensure it pays its fair share though that process is proving far more complicated than it sounds.
