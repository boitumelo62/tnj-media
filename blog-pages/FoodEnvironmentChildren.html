Lifestyle Food Environments Are Failing Children

September 14, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Obesity among children is no longer just a personal or family issue it's now a growing global crisis that needs urgent, collective action. According to a recent UNICEF report, obesity has officially overtaken underweight as the most common form of malnutrition in children and adolescents worldwide. This means that 1 in 10 children aged 5 to 19 around 188 million are now living with obesity, putting them at greater risk of serious health problems like diabetes, heart disease, and mental health issues later in life.

The report looked at data from over 190 countries, including South Africa, and revealed a concerning trend. While fewer children are underweight compared to the year 2000, obesity rates have more than tripled in the same time. In South Africa specifically, the percentage of overweight and obese children under five rose dramatically from 13% in 2016 to 23% in 2024. For older children (aged 5 to 19), it jumped from 9% in 2000 to 21% by 2022. These numbers reflect a larger shift in how food is produced, sold, and consumed globally. Experts agree that no single group is to blame. Instead, tackling childhood obesity requires coordinated efforts from governments, the food industry, schools, communities, and families.

Shaheda Omar from the Teddy Bear Foundation called the situation a “wake up call” for everyone involved. She stressed that governments need to take bold action for example, by regulating the marketing of junk food to children, taxing sugary drinks, and ensuring schools offer nutritious meals and education about healthy eating. One of the key causes of childhood obesity is the food environment. Children are constantly exposed to cheap, highly processed foods that are high in calories but low in nutrients. These foods are often heavily marketed especially to young people making it harder for families to make healthier choices. In lower income households, where access to fresh, healthy food is limited, families often rely on the most affordable and convenient options available, which are typically unhealthy.

Education plays a role too. Many parents and caregivers simply don’t have access to reliable information about nutrition or can’t afford better options even if they want to make healthier choices. This is where Dr. Chantell Witten, a dietitian, points out the role of poverty. In her view, the child support grant in South Africa falls below the poverty line and doesn’t give families enough to buy nutritious food. She also highlighted that obesity can start early, with formula-fed babies more likely to become overweight than those who are breastfed. The Department of Health is aware of the seriousness of the situation. Spokesperson Foster Mohale explained that while individual choices matter, it’s the environment around children that needs fixing. That means changing policies, improving food availability, making physical activity more accessible, and holding the food industry accountable for how they market to children.

Schools, too, have a part to play not just by banning junk food on their premises, but also by building a culture of healthy living. What’s clear is that fighting childhood obesity isn’t about pointing fingers it’s about acknowledging the shared responsibility and stepping up with real solutions. From stronger laws and public campaigns to better education and support for families, the message from health experts is unified, everyone has a role to play in protecting children’s health and creating a future where healthy choices are the easy ones.
