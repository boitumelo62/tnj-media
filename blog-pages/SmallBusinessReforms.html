Business Small Business Reforms Divided

September 15, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The South African government recently introduced two key legislative changes aimed at helping businesses operate more smoothly, especially small businesses. One of these changes has been welcomed, while the other has now been unexpectedly withdrawn. First, the Department of Employment and Labour, under Minister Nomakhosazana Meth, published a new code of practice on dismissals, which falls under the Labour Relations Act. This new code makes it easier for small businesses to let go of employees who aren’t performing well.

Unlike large companies that must follow complex and often costly procedures when dismissing someone, small businesses now have a simpler, less burdensome process to follow. Business Leadership South Africa (BLSA) CEO Busi Mavuso sees this as a positive step. She said the changes strike a fair balance by still requiring dismissals to be fair, but without forcing small businesses to jump through the same hoops as big corporations. She believes this move will encourage more people to start businesses and feel more confident hiring staff, knowing that managing underperformance won’t be so overwhelming.

The second piece of legislation, however, has hit a disappointing roadblock. The Department of Trade, Industry and Competition recently put forward draft amendments to the National Credit Act regulations. These changes were meant to make it easier for lenders to give small businesses access to loans, especially start ups. Under the current rules unchanged since 2013 even small businesses are treated like regular consumers. To get a loan, they must show things like payslips and bank statements to prove they can afford to repay, even if they have a solid business plan and strong future prospects.

Mavuso explained the problem with a simple example, if you're an entrepreneur who's been building a business idea for months and now need funding, your bank records might show no income so lenders legally can’t help you, even if your business idea is promising. The proposed regulations would have allowed lenders to look at the potential success of the business, not just past earnings. This could have made a big difference for start ups in South Africa. Unfortunately, the proposed changes were withdrawn before the public consultation period even finished. Mavuso called this a major setback. She said a lot of time and collaboration between government and business leaders had gone into developing these changes, and pulling the plug like this undermines all of that work.

She also pointed out that the roughly 20,000 public objections the minister received didn’t even relate to the changes that were being proposed.

Most of those objections came from students who feared they would be unfairly targeted by credit reporting rules. But those rules have been in place since 2006 and were not part of the proposed amendments at all. Mavuso criticized Minister Ebrahim Patel and Deputy Minister Fikile Majola’s decision to withdraw the amendments entirely based on these unrelated concerns, saying it was irrational and damaging to the entire policy making process. In her view, the government should have reviewed the feedback, addressed the student concerns separately if needed, and moved forward with improving access to credit for small businesses. Instead, the decision to scrap the proposal altogether has only delayed progress and harmed entrepreneurs who need better access to funding the most.
