Business Business Practices of Airlink Challenged in Competition Tribunal

The Competition Tribunal in South Africa is currently hearing a high profile case involving the airline Airlink, which has been accused of using predatory and excessive pricing tactics. These allegations go back to the period between 2012 and 2016, when Airlink operated on the Johannesburg–Mthatha route at the same time as a new airline, Fly Blue Crane, briefly entered the market. Aviation expert Guy Leitch described this case as potentially a turning point for the South African airline industry. According to him, the central issue is whether Airlink intentionally dropped its prices so low that Fly Blue Crane couldn’t compete and was eventually forced out of the market only for Airlink to raise prices again once the competition had disappeared.

The Competition Commission claims this move not only harmed consumers in the long run but also discouraged other new airlines from entering the market. The estimated financial damage from this situation could be as high as R108 million. Leitch pointed out that South Africa’s airline industry has been deregulated since the 1990s, which means there are no strict government controls over ticket pricing. Airlines are allowed to set prices based on demand, availability, and timing, which is known as dynamic pricing. This means tickets bought early tend to be cheaper, while last minute bookings cost more. In this open market, pricing strategies vary widely, and the line between competitive practice and anti competitive behavior can be blurry.

What makes this case especially complicated is that all airlines offer deals or price reductions at times sometimes just to fill seats or encourage travel on a particular route. For example, FlySafair, now the leading domestic airline in South Africa, often runs special offers. So the Tribunal now has to decide whether Airlink’s pricing was just aggressive marketing or a deliberate attempt to kill off a new competitor. Fly Blue Crane, started by former SAA and SA Express CEO Sizakele Mzimela, aimed to serve smaller, underserved routes like Mthatha. But it couldn’t survive, and one of the main reasons, according to the allegations, was that Airlink slashed its fares to unsustainable levels until Fly Blue Crane folded. Prices then allegedly rose again once the competition was gone.

Leitch also explained that flights to smaller towns like Mthatha are more expensive than routes between major cities, largely due to higher operational costs. The airport in Mthatha can only accommodate smaller 35 seat aircraft, unlike the larger planes that fly to Johannesburg, Durban, or Cape Town.

Despite carrying fewer passengers, the airline still has to cover the same fixed costs like fuel, crew, and maintenance spread across fewer paying customers. Still, Leitch acknowledged the crucial role Airlink plays in keeping Mthatha connected to the rest of the country. Without the airline operating four or five flights a day, the region could suffer economically. The nearest major airport is in East London, about 200 kilometers away, so losing air access would likely discourage investment and mobility.

The case is about more than just pricing it’s about finding the balance between fair competition and ensuring essential air services continue to places that need them. The Tribunal must now decide whether Airlink crossed the line and abused its market dominance, or whether it was simply playing by the rules of a tough and competitive industry.
