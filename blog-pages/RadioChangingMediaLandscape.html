Media Radio’s Role in a Changing Media Landscape

January 15, 2026 ● Tebuho Zongwana

Radio has always met disruption head on, adapting, evolving, and proving it’s far from dead. With MTV closing its doors at the end of 2025, we can finally retire “Video Killed the Radio Star” and focus on what really matters high rotation, fresh currents, and connecting with audiences.

South African radio has reached broadcast maturity. The SABC celebrated 89 years on air, community radio has grown over 21 years, and commercial radio carries a 40 year legacy. The industry is structured, with licensing, networks, regulators, and self regulation all in place, yet despite these frameworks, challenges persist.

Consolidation, staff cuts, and a focus on EBITDA over creativity risk turning radio into a rinse and repeat exercise. The question is simple where are we now, and where are we going? Regulation continues to test the sector. ICASA wrestles with digital transformation, licensing, and spectrum management, and tensions with community radio persist. Past licensing controversies highlight a need for clarity, and 2026 must see better outcomes for these stations, which are vital conduits for community dialogue. In commercial radio, disputes over playlisting and local quotas reflect wider friction with the regulator, showing the need for decisive action to unlock market opportunities.

Ministerial oversight remains uneven. The SABC Bill is still unresolved, the analogue switch off is in limbo, and government funding while critical requires strategic deployment to support public broadcasting. The SABC owes Sentech over R1 billion, yet it continues to deliver, showcasing the resilience of its teams. Cultural events and flagship stations like Metro FM demonstrate that commitment to audiences has not wavered. Digital platforms like SABC+ offer room for innovation, and untapped potential in mother tongue broadcasting could deliver both cultural and commercial benefits.

Commercial radio continues to drive research and innovation, essential for advertiser confidence. The Broadcast Research Council’s new methodology in 2026 will be crucial for rebuilding trust and ensuring all audiences, including rural and niche communities, are represented. Talent development remains a pressing challenge.

Initiatives like the Y Academy and HOT 1027 Academy nurture the next generation, but structured, formalized pathways are still lacking. Broadcasters must think seriously about where their future stars will come from and how to support them. Technology and AI will shape the next chapter of radio, but the human connection remains irreplaceable. Efficiency gains are welcome, but creativity, empathy, and storytelling will always define the medium.

Radio thrives when audiences come first, when multi platform content is fully supported, and when talent is nurtured. It thrives when we embrace resilience, relatability, and relevance. The story of radio isn’t about defending its existence it’s about celebrating why it continues to matter, innovate, and connect. In 2026, it’s time to play big, tell better stories, and prove that radio is alive, thriving, and as essential as ever.
