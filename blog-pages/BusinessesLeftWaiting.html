Business Businesses Left Waiting While Government Scrambles

August 4, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

The government has announced a vague and largely undefined set of support measures for businesses that are about to be hit hard by the new 30% tariff on exports to the United States, which officially comes into effect this week. While the announcement is meant to show that help is on the way, there’s growing concern that it’s too little, too late.

The Trump administration confirmed just last week that the tariff will go ahead starting 7 August 2025. This wasn't a surprise the tariff was first announced on 2 April but the South African government had been holding out hope that diplomatic talks and trade negotiations might soften the blow. That strategy failed. Over the past few weeks, it became clear that South African negotiators had been shut out of meaningful discussions with their US counterparts.

The government finally acknowledged, albeit reluctantly and at the last minute, that politics played a major role in the breakdown. Issues like black economic empowerment were reportedly major sticking points in the negotiations. These concerns had been raised informally by unofficial US delegations a month ago, but South Africa dismissed them at the time. Now, with time up and the tariff locked in, the Department of Trade and Industry has had to concede that these “political issues” helped land the country with one of the steepest penalties.

Even though talks with the US are expected to continue, and South Africa may still tweak its trade offer, the deadline has passed. The tariff is here, and the economic hit is already being felt. Businesses, especially in export-heavy sectors, are scrambling. Meanwhile, the government is now trying to play catch up, offering general promises of support but few concrete actions.

President Cyril Ramaphosa has said that “a number of measures” will be rolled out to help companies cope. He emphasized protecting exports and trying to redirect South Africa’s trade efforts toward African, Asian, and Middle Eastern markets. But beyond setting up an “Export Support Desk” for affected businesses to call, there’s little clarity on what this support will actually look like or when it will arrive.

Ramaphosa has promised that more detailed plans will be shared in time, including help for producers and workers now vulnerable due to the tariff. He also mentioned future plans to boost trade readiness and find new global markets for South African goods. But for many companies already feeling the financial damage, these long-term promises bring little comfort.

The Department of Trade, Industry and Competition says it had been preparing for the possibility of the tariff, but claims it couldn’t finalize any real plans until it had confirmation of the exact scope. Minister Parks Tau said that without knowing which industries would be hit, or by how much, it was impossible to build a proper response.

Now that the 30% figure is official, the government says it will intensify its trade missions abroad and help more local businesses get export-ready. These efforts, however, are expected to roll out “in the coming months,” while the consequences are already being felt today.

Business groups are not impressed. Busi Mavuso, the CEO of Business Leadership South Africa, has warned that the government must move much faster. Without immediate action, she said, the country faces a serious risk of manufacturing and farming businesses collapsing especially in vulnerable regions like the Eastern Cape, where industries like automotive manufacturing are deeply tied to exports.

Mavuso believes this is a full-blown crisis and has called for the establishment of a Trade Crisis Committee made up of both business leaders and government officials, including the National Treasury. She said this kind of coordinated effort similar to the responses seen during Covid-19, the 2021 riots, and the load shedding crisis is necessary to mount a real response.

She also urged the government to launch short-term financial support, such as loan programs and job protection measures, to stop factories from closing and workers from being laid off. She emphasized that the business sector has deep knowledge of what it takes to access new markets and what obstacles stand in the way and that government needs to listen and act alongside the private sector.

Ultimately, the message from business is clear: the current response isn’t fast or detailed enough. With the economic damage already underway, the country can’t afford delay.
