Tech News Empowering Creatives Through AI Literacy

November 7, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Artificial intelligence is everywhere these days, but true understanding of it is still rare. In the creative industries, the biggest challenge isn’t getting access to AI tools it’s about having the right context, the right skills, and the right people who can use AI not just to make content, but to make a real impact.

South Africa’s creative economy is full of potential. It contributes nearly 3% of the country’s GDP, employs close to a million people, and plays a key role in shaping innovation, identity, and growth. But as AI starts transforming everything from animation to post production, the real question is no longer whether creatives will use AI it’s whether they’ll be ready for it.

That’s why training has become more important than tools. Earlier this year, Johannesburg based Breadbin Productions launched the AI for Impact Academy, a first of its kind programme created not just to teach software, but to teach strategy. Developed with a Cape Town post production studio and supported by the National Film and Video Foundation, the Academy brought together a diverse group of learners from fresh graduates to experienced editors and showed them how to use AI as a true creative partner.

Instead of focusing on replacing human creativity, the training helped participants use AI to move faster, come up with bolder ideas, and explore new ways of storytelling. As Campbell from Breadbin explains, “We see AI not as a threat, but as a multiplier. It helps our teams and learners work faster, visualise ideas more clearly, and pitch braver concepts without waiting for a big budget.”

But this kind of mindset is still rare and that’s a problem. Research shows that 78% of South African organisations urgently need AI skills, but most can’t find qualified talent. Leaders expect that 40% of their workforce will need new skills within the next three years because of AI. And while many South Africans believe they know how to use AI properly, only just over half have had any formal or informal training. The skills gap is very real, and it’s growing.

What Breadbin’s Academy highlights is that AI literacy isn’t just technical it’s creative. It’s about understanding how tools fit into real workflows, how to use them ethically, and how to make sure they enhance rather than water down the creative process. By building this kind of understanding, the industry can strengthen its ability to adapt and grow.

This work is especially important now. With youth unemployment in South Africa at over 60% for those aged 15 24, and around 40% for those aged 25 34, digital training can be a powerful way to open new doors. Programmes across the country are already helping thousands of young people gain coding and AI skills, proving that there’s both a strong appetite and huge potential for digital growth.

Still, the creative economy needs more than coders. It needs storytellers, designers, editors, and animators people who know how to use AI not just to build systems, but to build meaning.

Breadbin’s Academy reminds us that the future of Africa’s creative economy won’t be shaped by the tools we have, but by the people we train, the stories we tell, and the courage we show in building something new. True innovation isn’t about keeping technology to ourselves it’s about sharing it and making it sustainable.

As AI continues to evolve, the creative industries must evolve too. Not by chasing every new trend, but by investing in people. When creativity is protected, when training is prioritised, and when technology is used with purpose, the result isn’t just better content it’s a stronger, more resilient creative economy. Africa’s next creative boom won’t come from tools. It will come from talent. And that begins with literacy.
