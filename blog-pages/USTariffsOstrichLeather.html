Business US Tariffs Risk Jobs at Home

August 26, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

US tariffs on South African products are starting to have ripple effects in unexpected places like the American cowboy boot industry. Arno Albertyn, who heads up the leather division at Klein Karoo International in Oudtshoorn, South Africa, says the new 30% tariff on ostrich leather exports from South Africa isn’t just hurting farmers and tanneries on his side of the world. It’s also putting pressure on American businesses that rely on this unique material especially cowboy boot manufacturers.

While only a fifth of South Africa’s ostrich leather exports go to the US, nearly 70% of the ostrich leather used by American manufacturers actually comes from South Africa. That’s a massive reliance on a single source. And now, that source is about to get a lot more expensive. Ostrich farming is no small deal in South Africa it’s a R1.2 billion industry that supports both meat and leather production. Albertyn explained that the sudden price increase caused by the tariffs doesn’t just affect the cost of leather; it compounds by the time the finished boots hit the shelves. It’s not as simple as adding a dollar or two. Once it gets through production and distribution, a $1 cost increase on raw material could translate to $50 or more added to the retail price.

And cowboy boots, especially those made from ostrich leather, are already a premium product. Some go for $400–$500, with high-end pairs reaching $5,000. For many Americans, though, these boots aren’t just fashion items they’re part of daily life. Ranchers, cowhands, and rural workers use them in the field, and they value the comfort, softness, and durability that ostrich leather offers.

Albertyn pointed out that while some wealthy buyers may absorb higher prices, most of the market is actually in the mid-range those $400 to $500 boots. And it’s that segment that’s most sensitive to price hikes. If those consumers decide the new prices are too steep, it could seriously hurt. The tariffs were meant to protect US industries. But in this case, they could end up doing the opposite. Cowboy boot manufacturing in the US is labour intensive; each pair takes around 200 workers to make.

And ostrich leather is only one component of the boot. Albertyn emphasized that South African suppliers aren’t taking jobs from the US they’re supplying critical raw materials that support American jobs.

For now, Klein Karoo has a buffer they managed to get several months’ worth of ostrich leather into the US before the tariffs took effect. That’s buying them time to figure out next steps with their American partners. But if the situation doesn’t change, price increases are inevitable.

As Albertyn sees it, the consequences are clear: if the tariffs remain in place, both South African farmers and American workers stand to lose. What was supposed to be a move to strengthen US industries might end up weakening them instead.
