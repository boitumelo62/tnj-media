Tech News South Africa Deploys Radioactive Technology in Groundbreaking Rhino Protection Effort

August 1, 2025 ● Tebuho Zongwana

In a bold and high-tech step to combat wildlife crime, South African scientists and conservationists have launched a pioneering initiative to deter rhino poaching by injecting rhino horns with trace amounts of radioactive isotopes.

The programme, spearheaded by the University of the Witwatersrand in collaboration with national nuclear regulators and wildlife protection groups, officially began on Thursday with the treatment of five rhinos. The radioactive markers, though harmless to the animals, can be detected by radiation scanners at international borders, potentially aiding law enforcement in intercepting illegal horn shipments.

James Larkin, the lead scientist on the project and head of the university’s Radiation and Health Physics Unit, confirmed that the isotopes pose no risk to the rhinos' health. “We’ve proven, scientifically and conclusively, that the method is safe and highly effective in making horns traceable through international customs systems,” Larkin said. He added that even minimal radiation levels were enough to trigger alarms in standard security equipment.

The concept was first trialled last year when approximately 20 rhinos at a sanctuary received similar injections. The success of that pilot study paved the way for Thursday’s broader rollout. Tests during the trials also demonstrated that horns could be detected even when hidden in full-sized shipping containers, a key advantage for border security.

The new initiative comes amid a growing crisis. The International Union for Conservation of Nature estimates that rhino populations have plummeted from 500,000 a century ago to around 27,000 today, largely driven by illegal horn trade. South Africa is home to about 16,000 rhinos, more than any other country, but faces severe poaching challenges, with an estimated 500 animals killed annually for their horns.

Authorities are encouraging both private rhino owners and conservation bodies to take part in the initiative by coordinating with the university to schedule isotope injections for their animals. Officials hope that as the project expands, it will significantly disrupt trafficking networks by making rhino horn a high-risk commodity to move across borders.
