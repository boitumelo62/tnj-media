Media When Convenience Became the Minimum and Trust Became the Differentiator

January 16, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

2025 brought a new level of intensity to customer experience and consumer behaviour. AI finally went mainstream, but customers stayed stubbornly human. They didn’t reward brands simply for adopting new technology; they rewarded those that solved problems quickly, fairly and with empathy. As satisfaction scores softened across many markets, the message became clear. The next era of customer experience will not be won by automation alone, but by trust, continuity across journeys, and thoughtful “human plus tech” design that delivers outcomes customers can actually feel.

Over the past year, convenience has shifted from being a differentiator to being the bare minimum. Consumers now expect speed and seamless service as standard. In South Africa, players like Checkers Sixty60 have reset expectations around delivery in much the same way Amazon once did in the US. Brands that combine fast fulfilment with flexible, no hassle returns are earning loyalty, while those that fall short are quickly left behind. At the same time, the expectation gap has widened.

Consumers are increasingly impatient with organisations held back by outdated systems. Digitally savvy, customer centric challengers, particularly in financial services, are thriving by connecting the dots and offering personalised, joined up experiences. Instant access is no longer a Gen Z expectation; it’s now universal.

AI has undoubtedly made customer service faster, but often at the expense of trust and human connection. While automation works well for simple tasks, many consumers still struggle to resolve complex issues without speaking to a real person. The 2026 Qualtrics Customer Experience Trends Report highlighted this tension clearly. Despite growing comfort with AI, nearly one in five consumers said AI based customer support delivered no benefit at all, a failure rate far higher than other AI use cases. The problem is not AI itself, but how it is being applied.

Trust has therefore become central, especially in South Africa. As scams grow more sophisticated and increasingly exploit trusted brands, consumers are more cautious than ever. Banks, mobile operators and insurers, in particular, are under pressure to prove authenticity and protect their relationships. Personalisation plays a major role here. Generic service no longer cuts it. Customers want to feel seen, understood and valued, and they reward brands that consistently deliver on this promise.

Interestingly, voice is making a comeback. After years of chatbots and self service portals, more consumers are returning to voice based interactions, even younger ones. While AI can help surface insights and understand pain points, survey fatigue is at an all time high. Automated metrics without meaning are no longer enough. Authentic, human conversations are becoming more valuable because they provide context, emotion and clarity that dashboards often miss.

Loyalty, too, has evolved. Points and rewards alone are no longer sufficient. Consumers want to feel genuinely heard. Brands that listen, respond and act on feedback are building deeper emotional connections. At the same time, resilience has become a defining factor in consumer behaviour. South Africans have adapted to challenges like load shedding, but ongoing issues such as water shortages and economic pressure are affecting disposable income, confidence and emotional tolerance.
