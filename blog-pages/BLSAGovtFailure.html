Business Businesses Are Paying the Price for Government Failure

August 11, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Busi Mavuso, the CEO of Business Leadership South Africa (BLSA), is making it clear: businesses can't thrive in a country that's falling apart. South Africa is showing worrying signs of being a "failed state" a term used when a government can no longer provide basic services or maintain control. In recent years, things like rolling blackouts (load shedding), failing infrastructure, water shortages, poor governance, and rising unemployment have painted a bleak picture of where the country is headed.

Despite this, many businesses have stepped in to support the government in trying to turn things around. But as Mavuso explains, that support comes at a heavy cost. Businesses are paying for things the government should be handling extra security, backup power, alternative transport and it's eating into resources that could be used to grow the economy. Still, companies know that if the country fails, they fail too. It's not about goodwill it's about survival.

BLSA plans to launch a reform tracker soon. This tool will monitor how government reforms are progressing and whether they're actually making a difference. According to Mavuso, real success should be judged by tangible improvements in the economy and a better environment for doing business.

However, she warns that the private sector can’t continue to fund and fix everything. Helping the state shouldn’t become a long-term burden that drains companies of money, people, and focus. Every rand used to patch up failing government systems is one that’s not being used for business growth, innovation, or job creation. Investors and shareholders are already questioning why businesses are spending so much to cover for government shortcomings. Mavuso emphasizes that business involvement in government functions must be selective and temporary. It shouldn't turn into a permanent crutch for the state.

Up to now, public-private partnerships have made real progress in areas like energy and logistics, and the country has started fixing long-standing problems like visa delays and outdated government systems.

But there needs to be a clear finish line. Businesses need goals, timelines, and evidence that their support is making a difference. That’s the purpose of the reform tracker BLSA is launching this week to ensure that efforts are focused, measurable, and ultimately, finite. As Mavuso puts it, this isn’t charity. It’s an investment and like any investment, it needs to pay off.
