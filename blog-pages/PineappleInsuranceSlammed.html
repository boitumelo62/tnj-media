Business Pineapple Insurance Slammed for ad, Ordered to Pull Campaign

August 6, 2025 ● Tebuho Zongwana

Pineapple Insurance has come under fire for a recent advertising campaign that sparked public outcry and was ultimately banned by the Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB) for promoting cultural stereotypes.

The campaign, which appeared on billboards and social media, attempted to use humour by comparing the risk of driving without insurance to greeting a taxi driver in the “wrong” language. The tagline read: “Driving without insurance is like saying ‘dumelang’ to a taxi driver” a nod to the Sotho greeting “dumelang,” often used in contrast to the Zulu greeting, “sawubona.”

Critics say the advertisement reinforced divisive tribal narratives by suggesting that greeting a taxi driver in a non-Zulu language could lead to hostility, drawing on a common stereotype about South African minibus taxi drivers, particularly in Gauteng, where many are Zulu-speaking.

The ARB confirmed that it received a formal complaint from a member of the public, who argued the message was culturally offensive and perpetuated tribal bias. After reviewing the ad, the regulator ruled that while humour in advertising is acceptable, it should never come at the expense of any cultural or ethnic group.

“The suggestion that using a certain language when speaking to a taxi driver is ‘risky’ reinforces dangerous generalisations,” the ARB noted in its decision. “Even if the intention was humour, the result is harmful.”

Pineapple Insurance defended the campaign, saying it was meant to reflect a shared South African experience and promote inclusivity through language diversity. The company claimed the concept was inspired by the linguistic dynamics of urban life, where miscommunication often happens in everyday settings.

However, the ARB found the rationale insufficient to justify the negative undertone of the message and ordered the insurer to withdraw the advert immediately from all platforms.

This incident has reignited debate about the thin line between cultural humour and harmful stereotyping in advertising. Experts say that while South Africans often use satire and language play to navigate diversity, marketers have a responsibility to tread carefully.

“This should be a learning moment for brands,” said media analyst Lunga Ndaba. “We can be witty without alienating or belittling entire groups of people. That’s where creativity must rise above lazy assumptions.” As of Wednesday (6 August, time of publish), Pineapple had not issued a formal apology, but all public instances of the campaign have been removed.
