Business The Strange New Trend of Apologising for Success

November 18, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Every few months, the marketing world seems to find a new fad to obsess over. Sometimes these trends feel fresh and genuine, the kind that make people smile or feel more connected to a brand. Other times, they’re confusing, irritating, and puzzling from a communication standpoint. Lately, the trend taking over is this habit of brands releasing dramatic, tongue in cheek apology letters not because they’ve done something wrong, but because they want to brag about how good they supposedly are.

It’s a strange mix of self-praise disguised as remorse, and it leaves many people wondering why this has become a thing in the first place. You’ve probably come across these posts. They’re hard to miss. A carefully crafted letter sits on an official looking template, almost like something you’d expect to see during a major corporate scandal. The tone is overly serious, with deliberate pauses and heavy, almost theatrical wording. The fonts are bold and gloomy. The headline declares, “We are sorry,” as if a major wrongdoing is about to be confessed. And then comes the twist that turns the whole message on its head: the brand isn’t apologising for a misstep at all.

Instead, it apologises for being too good, too efficient, too tasty, too popular essentially for being such a smashing success that people just can’t handle it.The whole thing loops back around every few months, almost like clockwork, making it feel less like creativity and more like everyone chasing the same moment of attention. While some marketing trends bring something refreshing or meaningful to the table, this one leans more toward forced humour and unnecessary theatrics. It’s the kind of communication that tries too hard to be clever, and in the process, becomes difficult to take seriously. The repetition doesn’t help either, because every version looks and sounds nearly identical to the last.

The overdone apology format, the faux dramatic tone, the predictable twist it all blends into a single, tired pattern. Instead of making brands seem witty, it often leaves the impression that they’re simply riding the wave of whatever is currently being copied online. Still, the trend keeps resurfacing, as if brands are afraid to miss out on a viral moment, no matter how contrived the idea has become. The irony is that these exaggerated apologies end up highlighting a larger issue: we’re seeing more branding that tries to mimic authenticity rather than actually being authentic.

And with each new wave of these “sorry for being amazing” messages, it becomes clearer that clever marketing isn’t about pretending to apologise for doing well it’s about understanding how to connect with people without leaning on recycled gimmicks.
