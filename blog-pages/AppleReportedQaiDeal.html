Tech News Apple’s Reported Q.ai Deal Signals Major Push into AI Driven Audio and Wearable Tech

Apple is reportedly making its second largest acquisition to date with a multibillion dollar move for AI audio startup Q.ai, marking a major step in its push toward more advanced voice and sensor driven computing. While Apple has not confirmed the purchase price, reports indicate the deal is valued at about 2 billion dollars, making it second only to the company’s Beats acquisition in 2014.

Q.ai is a four-year-old company focused on artificial intelligence powered audio and sensing systems. One of its most notable innovations involves optical sensor technology designed to detect subtle facial skin micro movements. This type of sensing could allow users to communicate with an AI assistant without speaking out loud, using tiny muscle signals and silent articulation instead of traditional voice commands.

The startup’s leadership team is also drawing attention. Q.ai CEO Aviad Maizels previously founded PrimeSense, the 3D sensing company Apple acquired in 2013. That technology later helped form the foundation of Face ID on the iPhone. With Maizels and other Q.ai founders expected to join Apple, industry watchers see a pattern of Apple buying specialized sensing firms and integrating their work into core products.

The technology could eventually support more natural and discreet interactions with Apple Intelligence features and a more advanced Siri. Possible future uses include AI enabled AirPods, smart glasses, Vision Pro devices, and other Apple hardware that benefits from low noise or silent input methods. It also suggests Apple is investing heavily in context aware computing that blends hardware sensors with generative AI.

In other news, Microsoft is facing mixed signals around Windows 11. Despite ongoing user complaints about reliability and performance, the company says the operating system has now surpassed one billion users, reaching the milestone faster than Windows 10 did. Still, criticism has continued as updates have introduced new bugs and instability.

Microsoft leadership says it is responding by shifting engineering teams into an internal repair phase known as swarming. During this period, teams focus less on new features and more on fixing core issues and improving performance. Executives say the priority for the coming months is stability, speed, and overall user experience, as Windows 11 expands across PCs, handheld gaming devices, and possibly future console platforms.
