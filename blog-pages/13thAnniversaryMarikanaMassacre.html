Media Government Criticised for Silence on 13th Anniversary of Marikana Massacre

August 18, 2025 ● Tebuho Zongwana

The South African government is facing mounting criticism for its silence as the nation marked 13 years since the Marikana Massacre, a tragedy in which 34 mineworkers were killed by police at the Lonmin Platinum Mine on 16 August 2012.

Families of the victims, labour unions, and civil society organisations expressed disappointment that no significant statement or commemoration came from government leaders to honour those who lost their lives. Critics argue that the silence reflects a lack of accountability and compassion for one of the most painful chapters in South Africa’s democratic history.

The Marikana Massacre remains a defining moment in post-apartheid South Africa, raising questions about police brutality, labour rights, and the state’s handling of industrial disputes. While inquiries and commissions have been held over the years, many believe that justice has not been served and that the families of the slain mineworkers continue to carry the burden without adequate redress.

Human rights activists have called for renewed efforts to ensure accountability and for 16 August to be formally recognised as a day of remembrance. “The silence from government dishonours the memories of the workers who died and deepens the wounds of their families,” one activist said.

As the country reflects on the tragedy 13 years later, the absence of official acknowledgement has sparked renewed calls for justice and for the state to fully confront the legacy of Marikana.
