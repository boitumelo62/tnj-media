Media The Innovation Dilemma Facing Today's Media Strategists

July 21, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Rethinking Traditional Advertising

Media

The Future of Advertising Is Prompted

There was a time when being a media strategist felt like being in the middle of a creative revolution. You weren't just buying ad space or checking boxes you were actively shaping the future, collaborating with new companies, imagining new uses for technology, and pitching bold, often unproven ideas to clients who were excited or at least curious about the possibilities.

It was experimental, collaborative, and yes, messy in all the right ways. But fast forward to today, and things feel different. The media world has consolidated. Most of the budget flows through a few dominant players Google, Meta, Amazon. These giants offer scale and safety, not necessarily surprise or originality. Their "innovations" are more about refinement than reinvention, and once they release a new offering, it's available to everyone at the same time. So where's the competitive advantage?

If you're a strategist today, you're navigating a system that rewards predictability and punishes risk. Want to bring in a new partner or platform? Be prepared to go through endless internal and external meetings. You have to sell the vision to your team, your client, their legal department, and their procurement arm. Even then, there's a decent chance the client won't bite.

Meanwhile, someone else is offering a plug-and-play solution from Meta that's ready to go and already has case studies and benchmarks. So can we blame strategists for not chasing every new idea? Not entirely. The environment makes innovation harder. But at the same time, if strategists only chase what's easy and proven, then yes over time, they become less strategic. They become operational. They stop stretching their thinking and their client's ambitions. And that's dangerous not just for the strategist, but for the industry at large.

Because once the work gets reduced to optimising existing tools and running safe plays, we're no longer solving problems we're just managing processes. And let's be honest: AI can manage processes. It can do targeting, allocation, optimisation. If that becomes the whole job, what's left for the human? The hope, as you point out, is that AI actually takes those tasks off our plates. If it works right, it could free strategists to be more creative, not less. To explore more new ideas, not fewer. But that requires a shift in mindset away from comfort and into curiosity again.

So are media strategists becoming innovation averse? Many are. Maybe not by choice, but by structure, pressure, and incentives. The question now is whether they can reclaim the space to be bold again or whether they'll fade into irrelevance as the machines quietly take over what used to be a uniquely human role.
