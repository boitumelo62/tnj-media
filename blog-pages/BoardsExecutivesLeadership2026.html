January 19, 2026 ● Uhone Kwinda

When Convenience Became the Minimum and Trust Became the Differentiator

South African companies are heading into 2026 with a much clearer understanding of how quickly disruption can reshape entire industries. The past few years have challenged long held assumptions about markets, supply chains, technology, and talent, often overturning them in a matter of months. As a result, boards and executive teams are increasingly asking hard questions about whether traditional leadership models are still fit for what lies ahead.

There is little reason to believe the environment will become calmer or more predictable anytime soon. The idea that recent disruption was a temporary phase, after which stability would return, no longer holds. Instead, leaders will need to adapt faster, make decisions with less certainty, and guide their organisations through ongoing change rather than occasional shocks.

From its work with boards and senior executives, Heidrick & Struggles observes several forces that are already shaping leadership agendas for 2026. Foremost among these is the continued rise of artificial intelligence. AI is no longer just a technology initiative it is a human and organisational challenge. Companies that treat it as a purely technical project, without investing in skills, mindset shifts, and cultural change.

The speed of digital change is adding further pressure. Automation evolved into digitisation, digitisation into AI, and AI into generative AI, with each transition arriving faster than the last. The next few years will be critical in setting expectations for how leaders invest, redesign roles, and use data at scale. When leadership teams lack a shared understanding of AI’s strategic impact, organisations often waste resources and struggle to course correct. Where that shared understanding exists, decisions are faster, investments are more focused, and technology becomes a genuine driver of long term value.

Boards are therefore placing greater emphasis on safeguarding leadership stability. Succession planning needs to be grounded in real, credible candidates, supported by deliberate internal development. Organisations that invest early in building internal pipelines and manage transitions decisively are more likely to maintain momentum through change. Those that rely too heavily on external hiring often face delays, uncertainty, and uneven leadership outcomes.

Looking ahead, boards are expected to focus less on individual roles and more on how leadership teams operate as a cohesive system. The organisations that sustain their advantage will be those where leaders are aligned, make high quality decisions quickly, and navigate uncertainty without losing strategic direction.
