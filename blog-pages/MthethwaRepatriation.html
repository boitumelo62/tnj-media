South African Ambassador Nathi Mthethwa Found Dead in Paris Hotel

The family of South Africa’s ambassador to France, Nathi Mthethwa, has confirmed that his remains will be brought back to South Africa once all official procedures are concluded.

Mthethwa was found dead this week outside a Paris hotel after he had checked into a room on the 22nd floor. French authorities are investigating the circumstances of his death, while also maintaining transparency and cooperation with the family, according to the family statement.

In the statement, the Mthethwa family said they had taken comfort from the compassion and unity shown by South Africans during this difficult time. They also expressed dissatisfaction with how they were informed of his passing. The family is awaiting clarity from ongoing investigations before making further public remarks.

Parliament’s international relations committee chairperson, Supra Mahumapelo, has called for a full and transparent investigation, stressing that speculation must be avoided. He said the government owes the Mthethwa family and the nation a clear account of what happened.

Senior provinces of the ruling party are expected to visit the Mthethwa family in KwaZulu-Natal this week to offer support and condolences. The late ambassador was a significant figure in government, having held roles including minister of police and minister of arts and culture.

Meanwhile, the Department of International Relations and Cooperation (Dirco) is working closely with the family and relevant authorities to coordinate the repatriation. But the minister has confirmed that South Africa will await the outcome of the French investigation before proceeding with formal repatriation arrangements.

French investigators have revealed that Mthethwa’s room window was found tampered with, and a note or message to his wife has been reported in which he expressed regret and intention to end his life. Authorities say no signs of a struggle or presence of drugs or toxins have been identified thus far.

A timeline compiled by local media shows that Mthethwa’s final known phone activity was around 3 p.m. on the day before his death. Later that evening, his wife received a distressing message. The next morning his body was discovered in the hotel’s courtyard at about 11 a.m.

At the time of his death, Mthethwa did not have a protection detail, a Dirco spokesperson confirmed. Ambassadors are not ordinarily provided with security.

Mthethwa’s death has sparked calls from experts for parallel investigations in South Africa, including making use of international cooperation tools to obtain evidence, witness statements, autopsy reports, and forensic materials. Some have urged that a domestic inquiry run concurrently with the French probe.

As the country mourns, attention now turns to the investigation’s outcome, the repatriation of his remains, and the accountability process ahead.

5pm News ● Madlanga Commission to Resume on 13 October After Nine Days of Testimony

The Madlanga Commission of Inquiry will resume its public hearings on 13 October after an adjournment caused by the illness of a key witness and the unavailability of one of the commissioners.

The inquiry, chaired by Justice Mandisa Maya Madlanga, has already sat for nine days, hearing explosive testimony from senior police officials and other witnesses. The proceedings have focused on allegations of political interference, the collapse of specialised policing units, and the existence of criminal syndicates operating within the security services.

On the opening days, former KwaZulu Natal police commissioner Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi gave detailed evidence, claiming that the Political Killings Task Team was deliberately dismantled to shield powerful individuals. He alleged that high ranking politicians and members of the security establishment had links to organised crime. Mkhwanazi’s testimony set the tone for the inquiry, which was established to investigate allegations of corruption and political interference within the police and intelligence community.

National Police Commissioner Fannie Masemola later appeared before the commission, where he was pressed on operational decisions and internal management of the police service. His testimony sought to distance the top leadership from political meddling, but questions remained over his role in controversial appointments and oversight.

Crime intelligence boss Lieutenant General Dumisani Khumalo followed with his own testimony, in which he described what he called the “Big Five” criminal syndicate. According to Khumalo, this group is tied to contract killings, drug trafficking and other forms of organised crime. His appearance was cut short when he fell ill on the third day of his evidence, forcing the commission to adjourn.

Complicating matters further, commissioner Sesi Baloyi informed the inquiry that she would be unavailable between 6 and 10 October due to her commitments on the Judicial Service Commission. This scheduling clash, together with Khumalo’s illness, led to the announcement that the hearings will resume on 13 October.

The nine days of hearings have already produced significant revelations, including the late minister Nathi Mthethwa being named in testimony as a figure linked to political interference. His sudden death left questions over whether he would have been called to testify before the commission.

So far the inquiry has been marked by both political reaction and public expectation. Opposition parties have welcomed the revelations, while some groups have warned that the process will only matter if it leads to real accountability.

When proceedings resume later this month, the commission is expected to recall Khumalo to complete his evidence. More high profile witnesses are lined up, with the inquiry set to run for several months.
