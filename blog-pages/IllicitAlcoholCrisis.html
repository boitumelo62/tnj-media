South Africa’s illegal alcohol trade has grown into a R25-billion industry, operating out of sight and causing serious harm to both public health and the economy. A new report by Euromonitor, titled Understanding the Illicit Alcohol Market in South Africa, reveals the extent of the problem and how it's impacting the country.

The study estimates that the government is losing about R16.5 billion in tax revenue every year due to illicit alcohol. This underground market is thriving in an environment shaped by weak law enforcement, financial hardship, and widespread alcohol addiction. According to the research, around 18% of all alcohol consumed in South Africa is illicit. That means roughly one in every five drinks consumed in the country is illegal.

Illicit alcohol includes counterfeit products, alcohol that bypasses legal duties, and homemade or illegally manufactured concoctions. Because these products aren’t made or sold under regulated conditions, they often pose serious health risks. Tests on illicit alcohol have found dangerous chemicals that can lead to severe health complications or even death.

Dr. Shamal Ramesar of the Drinks Federation of South Africa confirms that some of the substances found in these drinks are extremely harmful. Despite these risks, many consumers are unaware of the dangers. The study shows that 83% of people don’t realize that drinking counterfeit alcohol could damage their health. Nearly half of those surveyed know someone who suffered serious health effects from drinking illicit alcohol, and almost a third know someone who died because of it.

This issue doesn’t just affect individuals—it also creates an unfair competitive environment for legitimate alcohol producers and sellers. Legal businesses are forced to compete with illegal operators who can offer much cheaper prices because they don’t pay taxes or comply with regulations. Richard Rivett-Carnac, CEO of South African Breweries, points out that illicit alcohol is often sold at more than 30% less than legal alternatives. With many consumers struggling financially, it’s not surprising that some are turning to the cheaper but far riskier option.

Hardin Ratshisusu from the National Consumer Commission is urging consumers to report suspicious alcohol products and encouraging retailers to educate themselves to avoid unknowingly supporting the illegal trade. The growth of the illicit alcohol market is not just a financial loss for the country—it’s a serious public health issue that demands urgent attention.
