Business Eskom’s Tariffs Create Severe Burden as Demand Falls Off a Cliff

October 20, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Eskom has raised electricity prices so sharply over the years that many South Africans simply can't afford to keep up. This has caused a major drop in demand for its electricity, and promises of future price stability are coming far too late to fix the damage. Independent energy analyst Pieter Jordaan studied how Eskom’s price hikes have affected people’s ability to pay for electricity. He focused on something called the “energy burden,” which looks at how much of a household’s income goes toward electricity.

If more than 6% of income is spent on power, that’s considered high. More than 10% is considered severe. Once people hit those thresholds, they start cutting back, using less power, or switching to cheaper alternatives like gas, solar, or even firewood. Jordaan compared low income households earning around R5,000 a month with middle income households earning about R22,500 after tax. Back in 2008, middle income households were paying slightly less of their income toward electricity than low income households.

But after two rounds of drastic tariff changes first in 2008/09 and now again in 2025 middle income families are suddenly under more pressure. In fact, their energy burden jumped from 3.5% in 2008 to 10.9% in 2025, which is well into the “severe” zone. That means Eskom’s prices are now hitting middle class households even harder than the poor, which is unusual and dangerous for the utility’s future. And it’s not over yet. Only a small part of the 2025 tariff restructuring has been implemented, with much steeper increases still on the way in 2026 and 2027.

Government officials, including Energy Minister Kgosientsho Ramokgopa, have tried to reassure the public by promising an end to double digit increases. But Eskom was already on track for smaller increases around 9% with or without government involvement. The truth is, by now, it doesn’t matter. The damage has been done. Jordaan explains that two massive price hikes in 2008 and 2009 combined with the global financial crisis completely flattened demand for five years. Eskom tried to reverse course in 2011 by cutting prices slightly, but it was too late. Even when economic growth returned in 2014–2016, the demand only picked up briefly, then started falling again. And from 2019 onward, household electricity use has been dropping by about 5% per year.

That’s not because people suddenly became more energy efficient. It’s because they’re either cutting back drastically or leaving the grid entirely. Even now, with load shedding temporarily out of the picture, electricity demand remains far below what it used to be. Jordaan says that Eskom has entered what he calls “demand quicksand” a situation where its own pricing has pushed customers away permanently, and there’s no clear way out. What’s even more alarming is how overpriced Eskom’s electricity has become. Jordaan calculates that a fair price for middle income customers in 2025 should be about R2.11 per kilowatt hour. But they’re currently paying R4.11 almost double. For low income households, a fair price would be R1.57, yet they’re being charged R2.49.

In real terms, electricity prices have gone up 937% since 2007, while inflation has only increased by 155%. That kind of difference is simply unsustainable. At this point, Jordaan argues, Eskom can no longer survive on its own. The only thing keeping it afloat is constant financial support from the South African taxpayer. Without drastic and immediate changes, Eskom is stuck and dragging its customers down with it.
