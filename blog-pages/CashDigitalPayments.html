Tech News Cash Still Holds Strong as South Africa Embraces Digital Payments

November 11, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

South Africa is seeing a major rise in digital payments as banks continue to promote systems like instant EFT. Despite this growing shift toward digital transactions, cash still plays a big role in the daily lives of many South Africans. People are finding new ways to get physical money, especially as banks reduce the number of ATMs across the country. Instead of heading to a bank, more South Africans are now drawing cash from retailers and till points.

The new PayInc Cash Index shows that although both the supply and demand for cash have eased slightly over the past year, the numbers remain fairly steady. This suggests that notes and coins continue to be resilient, especially for smaller, everyday purchases. The index tracks how physical money moves through the economy, using data from PayInc’s Independent Cash Management Service to monitor cash inventories, how much cash is being supplied to access points, and overall demand.

According to the South African Reserve Bank, the total value of notes and coins in circulation has remained stable since 2020. By 2024, about R180 billion in cash was circulating roughly 2.5% of the country’s GDP. However, when adjusted for inflation, the real value of that cash has decreased somewhat. PayInc notes that banks have been holding less cash over the past few years, but this could be more about improving efficiency than a real drop in demand.

What’s changing is where people get their cash. Instead of relying only on banks and their ATMs, more South Africans are using independent ATM networks, cash services run by retailers, and other non bank providers. PayInc describes this as a structural shift in the cash ecosystem, not a sign that cash is becoming less important. Their data shows fewer ATMs run by major banks but an increase in independent networks and more cashback and deposit options at stores.

It’s harder to track what happens to cash once it leaves the banking system, especially now that so many non-bank players are involved. To get a clearer picture, the PayInc Cash Index compares the total amount of cash in circulation with the cash held by the Reserve Bank and commercial banks. The difference called “other cash” represents money held by individuals and non bank providers. This gap helps show how much cash people and businesses are keeping outside the formal banking system.

Economist Elize Kruger points out that even though banks are shutting down ATMs, it doesn’t mean South Africans are using less cash. Instead, people are simply getting it from different places, such as retail stores, which now play a bigger role in the cash supply chain. Banks are adapting to this change too. For example, Standard Bank now offers voucher based instant payments that can be redeemed at more than 400,000 withdrawal points across the country.

At the same time, the rise in digital payments is undeniable. Systems like PayShap and EFT have grown rapidly from about 406 million transactions in 2010 to over 1.1 billion in 2024. This clearly shows that South Africans are embracing digital payment options more than ever before.

Overall, the data suggests that while cash continues to hold a strong place in the economy, the way people access and use it is evolving. The cash ecosystem is modernising, with more choices and improved efficiency, even as digital payments become a larger part of everyday life.
