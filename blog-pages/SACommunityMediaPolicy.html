November 14, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Community Media in Africa Is Evolving

Community media in South Africa have received support for about 30 years, yet access to information remains highly unequal. Media availability still mirrors the country’s deep socio-economic divisions. People in middle-class suburbs have a wide range of news sources at their fingertips, while poorer communities and rural areas often have almost none.

Support for community media has helped expand media outlets in marginalised places, with around 230 licensed community radio stations and nearly 190 community print or online publications. But many of these outlets struggle to stay afloat. Most media activity is still concentrated in cities, provincial hubs and other centres of political or economic power.

In a recent policy study, the authors looked at how post-apartheid efforts to give space to marginalised voices in the media have unfolded. The research reviewed what has been achieved over the past three decades and what needs to change. It found that policy has often been confused, poorly coordinated and influenced by political tensions, especially between the government and mainstream media. Weaknesses in state institutions meant to support community media, such as the Media Development and Diversity Agency, have further limited progress.

Community television has suffered even more from these policy gaps. Because TV is expensive to run, people have long debated whether it can work as a non-profit community service or whether it needs commercial or regional public-service models to survive. The slow, poorly managed transition to digital terrestrial television has added to the struggle, with operators warning that their needs were largely ignored.

Many community outlets have been pushed into commercial behaviour simply to survive, drifting away from the original idea of community-centred media. Government advertising, although never enough, has become a crucial source of income, leaving some outlets vulnerable to political pressure. One striking example involved a mining-town newspaper whose mayor tried to buy it. When that failed, he attempted to start a competing paper and later cut off municipal advertising, forcing the original publication to shut down for several years until local politics shifted.

Policy discussions have increasingly grouped community media together with debates about online platforms and new technologies. The most recent draft White Paper from July 2025 claims to aim for universal access to high-quality, relevant content, but it pays little attention to the harsh economic realities in the poorest communities. While technology is evolving quickly, the report argues that any meaningful policy should start from the principle of citizens’ right to information and a realistic understanding of unequal access.

The study proposes that South Africa adopt a clear policy centred on reducing information inequality. It suggests reviewing all existing policies and institutions to ensure they truly support communities on the margins, including overhauling the Media Development and Diversity Agency. It recommends creating better opportunities for independent local media to access market and government support, such as training funds and new funding streams from digital platforms.

It also argues that government communication budgets should benefit independent local media in a fair and transparent way. Finally, it proposes a type of basic income grant for media outlets in the most economically depressed areas. These local economies simply cannot sustain news providers on their own, and expecting them to do so is unrealistic. A long-term subsidy would be needed, though careful planning would be required to design such a system effectively.
