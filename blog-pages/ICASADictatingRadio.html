Communication Is a Strategic Advantage, Not a Side Function

Hot 102.7 FM is currently rallying its listeners to support an online petition asking ICASA to drop the rule that forces the station to broadcast 50% classical music. It’s an unusual and, frankly, impractical requirement. It’s like giving a fast food restaurant a licence, then insisting it also serve fine dining in equal measure. Of course, classical music lovers won’t like the idea of losing that airtime, but if there truly is a demand for a dedicated classical station, then ICASA should find a frequency and let someone who believes in that format invest in it.

I’ve long felt that South Africa’s broadcasting regulator should avoid dictating formats. If the intention is to build a more vibrant and competitive commercial radio sector, then this level of control works against that goal. The industry has been held back for years by heavy handed regulation and an almost paternalistic approach from ICASA. While this has benefited some established players especially former SABC stations, Primedia, and those who secured new licences in the late ’90s it’s made it harder for listeners to enjoy variety and for new competitors to enter the market. It has also allowed big stations and groups to dominate revenue, creating high barriers for newcomers.

The decision to force HOT to play 50% classical music shows a real misunderstanding of how listeners engage with radio. People choose stations based on identity and consistency. Trying to fold classical music into a brand built on Old Skool and R&B simply doesn’t work. To cope with the rule, HOT separated the genres by airing classical music from 19:00 to 05:00. It was the only workable solution, but it came with an obvious downside: at 19:00, listeners switched to other stations for contemporary music, and many wouldn’t necessarily return the next morning. They might enjoy what they found elsewhere, and that puts HOT at a clear disadvantage.

This entire situation circles back to a bigger issue South African commercial radio isn’t as competitive as it should be. Back in 1995, I wrote an article for the Saturday Star arguing that the IBA, now ICASA, needed to apply its own “RDP of the airwaves.” Decades later, the argument still holds. In countries like the United States, radio thrives because stations compete within format categories. Listeners benefit from real choice, and stations have to work harder to stay relevant, produce engaging content, and market themselves smartly. Dominant stations still exist, but the listener always has options, and that’s healthy.

This is how a free market system should function once a format becomes successful, others enter the space to claim a portion of that market. If it becomes too crowded, the format naturally shifts or consolidates. There’s nothing wrong with that. In fact, in South Africa it would allow advertisers more choice, give listeners real power, and push stations to innovate. It might even spark interest in launching stations in smaller markets.

ICASA often responds by saying the FM spectrum is limited. I don’t buy that. In the early 1990s, New York City had more than 100 FM stations within a 30-km radius. If ICASA studied how other countries manage their frequency spectrum, it could create far more space for commercial broadcasters here.

Back in the mid-90s, when the IBA began allocating licences, the focus was on community stations. Some were genuinely community driven, but others operated as commercial stations disguised as community broadcasters. Even if there are solid arguments for community radio, the IBA placed many of these stations in parts of the FM spectrum that could have been better managed.

In my 1995 article, I explored the British model of dividing and organising FM frequencies, and suggested South Africa should take a similar approach. The core idea remains relevant today better spectrum management, less interference in formats, and more trust in the market would create a healthier, more diverse, and more competitive radio landscape one that serves listeners first.
