Media Traditional Newsrooms Face a Creator Led Future

August 5, 2025 ● Uhone Kwinda

Last December, the Knight Centre for Journalism in the Americas launched a course called How to Be a Trusted Voice Online, aimed at digital content creators and journalists. Over 9,000 people from 172 countries signed up, which was a clear sign of how many individuals are actively shaping the online information space today. It also reflected something deeper: from the public’s perspective, the distinction between traditional journalists and independent creators is becoming increasingly hard to see.

This shift hasn’t gone unnoticed by newsrooms. Many recognize that audiences are spending more time on social platforms, engaging with content that feels personal, authentic, and often led by creators rather than institutions. That shift has led traditional media to explore new ways of connecting, but the path forward is still murky. Some outlets are testing fresh ideas, though not always with consistent results.

A prominent example of this experimentation was The Washington Post’s “third newsroom,” which was eventually rebranded as WP Ventures. Set up apart from the main newsroom but overseen by top editors, it focused on newer formats like social video, newsletters, and direct audience engagement. For a while, it seemed like a smart, forward-looking move, and according to Editor Krissah Thompson, it did see some success. But recently, the Post changed direction. WP Ventures will no longer function as an editorial project it’s now part of the commercial side, reporting to the chief strategy officer.

Samantha Henig, who led the initiative editorially, will now serve as its general manager. Thompson, one of the project’s champions, is leaving the Post as part of a buyout. Others are leaving too. Dave Jorgenson, known for his viral TikToks for the Post, is departing along with two colleagues. They’re starting their own company, Local News International, aiming to speak to audiences who feel alienated or confused by today’s news landscape.

Meanwhile, observers like Ryan Kellett, a former audience editor and current Nieman Berkman fellow, are closely watching this evolution. Speaking at the World News Media Congress, Kellett predicted a continued breakaway from traditional newsroom roles, as more journalists choose to become independent creators.

In a recent report co-authored with Ben Reininga, What Legacy Newsrooms Can Learn From Social Media Creators, Kellett argues that the traits most valued by today’s audiences are trust and relatability the feeling that they’re hearing from someone like them. This raises a key question: should traditional newsrooms start hiring non-journalist creators to keep up?

Kellett acknowledges that many publishers are wary, especially when it comes to issues like brand safety. His suggestion: work with creators who bring real expertise academic voices, for instance if hiring influencers feels like too much of a stretch.

His message to legacy media is clear: either adapt to the creator model, or risk becoming irrelevant. He even lays out a simple approach: first, formalize a program to support in-house staff who are comfortable working as creators; second, make sure the creator’s style fits the brand without diluting either one; and third, invest in a dedicated team that uses creator-led storytelling to reach new audiences.

Still, partnerships with freelance creators aren’t always smooth. Creator and journalist Sophia Smith Galer points out that newsrooms often don’t offer fair compensation or the kind of support creators are looking for. Many outlets also lack the social media presence needed to boost a creator’s visibility, making the deal less appealing. In short, creators are interested in partnerships but only when they’re treated fairly and offered real value.

Despite these challenges, successful collaborations are happening. One example is journalist Enrique Anarte’s work with MediaWise, a media literacy initiative by the Poynter Institute. Projects like this suggest that, when done right, the intersection of journalism and creator culture can offer real impact. But for traditional newsrooms, embracing this new reality means more than just experimenting it means being willing to change.
